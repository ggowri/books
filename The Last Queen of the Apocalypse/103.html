Chapter 103 Agricultural-Industrial Price Gap

This meant her base had finally survived the harshest early stage.

Before, Iris had been using her own stockpile to support everyone. Now, the base was starting to sustain itself—and support her in return.

Massive amounts of produce flowed into Iris's warehouse through the point exchange system. She settled their labor with points, then put daily necessities up for residents to buy back with those same points.

No matter how much they exchanged, they could never spend all their points. The leftover points became their accumulated wealth.

In this resource-scarce apocalypse, residents could only produce food. Using the agricultural-industrial price gap, Iris perfectly controlled the points in circulation.

The gap was simple: keep the price of farm goods low, and industrial goods sky-high.

For instance, she bought grain from residents at 1 point per pound, and sold a single new piece of clothing for 100 points.

It wasn't an equal trade in reality, but that was how she ran it.

By keeping grain cheap and manufactured goods expensive, she steadily drained excess points from residents, balancing their labor output.

They could only grow food. Everything else they had to buy from her.

Even if clothes were expensive, once they saved enough points, they still bought them.

Of course, the people of Midsummer Base still benefited enormously.

While survivors at other bases starved and fought to stay alive, Midsummer residents ate their fill and could trade extra for clothes, shampoo, snacks, and drinks.

It was almost unthinkable in the apocalypse.

As the base developed and residents kept producing, minor inflation naturally occurred. Iris handled all the calculations herself, adjusting prices constantly to match the territory's resource levels.

This kept everyone living in relative comfort and stability.

Around the same time, Iris's piglets began breeding too.

Once sweet potatoes and other crops were harvested, she released a batch of young pigs for residents to raise.

With labor still short, building a dedicated, managed farm would waste manpower and resources.

Besides, directly-run pigs were harder to motivate people to care for, and she'd have to prepare feed separately.

So she distributed piglets household by household.

Each piglet was weighed when adopted. When fully grown, they were weighed again.

Residents earned 10 points per pound of pork, plus kept 10% of the meat.

This set off a frenzy of enthusiasm.

They worked harder to grow more grain, fed the pigs scraps every day, and checked on them constantly, terrified something might happen to them.

By sharing benefits, she also shared risk.

Everyone's sense of belonging to the base grew stronger.

This was their land, their livestock. They weren't leaving.

With stable crop output and livestock now added, the base's prosperity skyrocketed.

It rose bit by bit, until it finally broke 500, unlocking the title: Suburban Area.

The next tier would be Small Town at 1000 prosperity.

Iris usually didn't care about the system's silly little titles. But this time, she got a huge surprise.

Resident daily point contributions doubled.

Every resident in her territory, besides giving a one-time 100 points when they first joined, contributed 1 system point per day. With nineteen residents, that added up to 19 points daily.

Even without going out to fight zombies or expand her territory, the points kept piling up steadily.

Now that her prosperity had reached Suburban Area, each resident contributed 2 points per day. Nineteen residents meant 38 points.

It might sound small, but these points added up fast. With a high enough level and more residents, Iris could earn massive points every single day just by doing nothing.

As development continued, the passive income from residents would only get bigger.

Her long-held goal—the Zombie Virus Inhibitor—was no longer a distant fantasy.

Once she realized daily point contributions scaled with prosperity, Iris felt far more secure.

They had to keep growing.

But she also knew clearly: Her base was still a small sapling. It couldn't withstand a real storm yet.

She had to protect it carefully.
