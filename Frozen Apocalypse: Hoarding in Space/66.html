Chapter 65

"Oh my God, the supplies are not enough and they can't even arrive on time."
"How are we supposed to survive..."
"What are we supposed to eat tonight?"

The crowd erupted in complaints at the news.
"The big pots upstairs are available. Anyone with rice can cook together and share it equally."

That was the only solution Mrs Harris could think of.
People accepted the bad news and stopped grumbling.
When they moved into the basement, most families had brought their household food stores. They still had supplies.

Now, families gathered together and began discussing how much rice each would contribute to cook a pot of congee.

Callum fished a little measuring cup out of his basement. "How about this? One cup of rice gets you one big bowl of congee. Two cups gets you two bowls."
"That works! Anyone have a pen? We'll keep a record and divide it up later."

Someone in the crowd agreed. Everyone approved of the method.
Mrs Harris sat at the table used for daily meal distribution. A large basin sat beside her.
Each family measured out their rice in the little cup. Mrs Harris recorded the amount and tipped the rice into the basin.

A very small number of families had no stored rice at all. They lurked at the back of the crowd, eyes gleaming green, staring at the basin.
They would have loved to rush forward and grab all the rice. But four security guards stood around the basin. No chance.

Edward joined in. He still had the rice he had stolen from Mr Finch.
He poured one cup of rice into the basin. One large bowl of congee – he would drink half, and Anna and Maggie would share the rest.

The workers on duty today carried a small half basin of rice upstairs to the ground floor.
Half an hour later, two large basins of thin, watery hot congee were brought back down.

The starving crowd stared at the two basins. No one stepped forward.
"Come and get your food! Hurry up!"

Mrs Harris banged on the basin.
"All the rice is at the bottom. That's just rice water on top. No way I'm going first."
"Right. Who doesn't want the rice at the bottom?"

Everyone muttered. They all wanted a proper meal.
Mrs Harris stirred the serving ladle vigorously through the basin, bringing the rice grains from the bottom to the top. It looked much more even now.
"Is that good enough? Come on!"

Mrs Harris shouted at the crowd impatiently. She was fed up with waiting on this bunch of entitled lot every single day.
Seeing that the congee was indeed more uniform, the crowd finally came forward for their servings.

Edward carried a large full bowl of hot congee back to the basement.
"Why is the food so late today, Dad? Hurry and give me some. I'm starving."

Anna had grown impatient waiting.
Edward found two empty bowls. He kept half for himself and divided the remaining half between the two bowls.
Without a word, he handed one bowl to Anna.

"How is that supposed to fill me up?"

Even handcuffed, Anna hadn't lost her spoiled temper.
"Don't want it?" If she dared refuse, Edward would drink it himself.
"No, no. I want it."

Anna saw her father reach for the bowl and quickly agreed.
She took the bowl, ignored how hot it was, and gulped it down. Two mouthfuls and it was gone.

Maggie was leaning against the cold basement wall, resting with her eyes closed.
It was less resting and more enduring the pain through gritted teeth.
She had no strength. It hurt to open her eyes. It hurt to open her mouth. So she just sat still and felt the pain throbbing along her nerves.

Edward put Maggie's small portion of congee on the floor. "Time to eat."
Then he sat on his own bed and slurped down the hot congee.

In the corner of the underground car park, Daniel smelled the congee aroma filling the air and swallowed hard.
He had stolen only biscuits and instant noodles. He hadn't taken any rice.
Just hold on. At 7 o'clock, when hot water was available, he would soak half a noodle block.

"Does anyone have paracetamol?"
"My child has a fever! Does anyone have paracetamol?"
"Please, I beg you. Sell me some paracetamol!"

A cry cut through the quiet of the basement. Each sentence grew louder. People curled up in their own beds couldn't help looking in that direction.
A woman around thirty years old held a boy of four or five in her arms. She looked at the crowd in panic.
The crowd answered her only with silence.

Mrs Harris had just finished her bowl of congee when she heard the commotion in the basement.
She sighed, walked over, and checked. "What's wrong?"
"Do you have paracetamol? My child has a very high fever. Does anyone have paracetamol?"

The mother was crying in desperation.
Mrs Harris was in a difficult position. People got sick in the basement every single day. The little medicine in the medical kit had run out long ago.
"We don't have any here. Does anyone have paracetamol? Even if you'd trade it for something, the child's mother can swap."

Silence.
Mrs Harris had no choice but to pick up the megaphone and walk further into the basement.
"Anyone have paracetamol? A child has a fever. Someone wants to trade with you."

The announcement repeated several times. Not a single tightly closed basement door opened.
Sophie looked at the stack of books on her desk. She hesitated for a while, clenched her fist, and did not reach out.
Hidden among those books were some fever reducers and antibiotics.
She wanted to help. But she was too afraid.
It wasn't that she couldn't spare one pill. She was afraid of losing her life.

Leo also heard the loudspeaker outside his door. He hesitated for a moment, then decided not to get involved.
But the two way radio beside his hand crackled. "Leo. Leo. Did you hear that? A child has a fever."

Callum's deliberately lowered voice came through the radio.
"Mind your own business." Leo pressed the button on the radio and replied with only those four words.

Callum looked at the ibuprofen in his palm and tucked it back into his pocket.

Late at night, everyone had fallen asleep. A scream tore through the darkness.
"Ah! My child!"

Her child was dead. Heat shock.
The people around woke up. Some lay stiffly in place. Others rustled and sat up.
"Turn on the lights! Turn on the lights!"

Someone shouted a couple of times. The night shift security guard, half asleep, sat up from the table and flicked the switch.
"Mrs Harris. Mrs Harris. Wake up. That child is dead."

The guard didn't know what to do, so he woke the person with the most authority.
Mrs Harris's loud snoring was interrupted. "Huh? What?"
She wasn't fully awake yet. Her brain took a moment to catch up.
"Dead?" Her heart jolted. She had been a mother herself. She understood that pain all too well.

Mrs Harris heaved her bulky body upright, stumbled, and hurried toward the woman.
The woman knelt on the floor, holding her pale, silent child in her arms, wailing.
Mrs Harris came up and put an arm around the woman's shoulders. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

A few kind souls drifted over and offered a few words of comfort.
Most people stayed in their beds, bodies rigid, pretending they hadn't been woken.
They felt guilty and ashamed. But everyone valued their own life more.

They sat like that until morning. The child never recovered.
