Chapter 91

"Alright, everyone. Settle down and get some rest."

Mrs. Harris dusted off her hands and was the first to get moving, unloading blankets from the sled.

The others followed suit, unpacking their own gear.

Their bedding was spread directly on the ground. Every sleeping spot was arranged as close to the fire as possible. It was the only way to stay reasonably warm through the night.

"You've all had a hard day," Mrs. Harris said. "We'll take turns on fire watch. One hour each. Under no circumstances can this fire go out. When your hour's up, wake the next person. Anyone not on duty tonight will take a shift tomorrow."

She pointed at Leo.

Leo nodded. An early shift was far better than being dragged awake in the middle of the night.

The fire burned steadily through the first half of the night.

Eventually, it was Maggie's turn.

She had worked harder than almost anyone during the day. While others had only carried blankets and food, she'd also been helping support another person.

She was sleeping deeply when someone beside her whispered, "Your turn. Wake up."

They called her name three times.

No response.

Finally, the person pinched her arm hard.

Maggie jerked awake.

"Huh? What's going on?"

Still groggy, she couldn't make sense of her surroundings.

"It's your watch. Keep an eye on the fire."

After spending several minutes waking her, the previous guard crawled into his blanket and was asleep almost immediately.

Maggie rubbed her eyes.

The fire was burning brightly.

Looks fine, she thought.

I'll close my eyes for a few minutes and wake the next person later.

The next time she opened her eyes, someone was kicking her.

Hard.

"What is wrong with you? You fell asleep!"

Maggie blinked in confusion before remembering she was supposed to be on watch.

She bolted upright.

"Oh! I must've dozed off by accident. The fire didn't go out, did it? It couldn't have gone out in just one hour."

She sounded genuinely unconvinced.

Everyone nearby was awake now.

Wrapped tightly in blankets, shivering from the cold, they glared at her.

"No, it didn't go out in one hour," snapped the person she'd relieved. "It went out because you slept for three hours and never woke the next person."

"I did my shift properly," he continued angrily. "The fire was fine when I handed it over."

Maggie bristled.

"So now you're blaming me for everything?"

"Good grief, Maggie!" the man exploded. "You never woke the next person! That's the whole point! How are you not getting this?"

Maggie's confidence wilted immediately.

"Well... I didn't mean to. I accidentally fell asleep."

"Enough."

Mrs. Harris's voice cut through the argument.

"The fire's been relit. Everybody get some sleep. We've got another long day ahead tomorrow."

She climbed back into her blankets.

Rest was too valuable to waste on pointless bickering.

The others reluctantly let the matter drop.

Several shot Maggie furious looks before returning to their bedding.

Anna had woken during the commotion.

Seeing her mother's pathetic excuse-making, she rolled her eyes, turned over, and went right back to sleep.

"Maggie," Mrs. Harris said sternly from inside her blankets, "everyone else took their responsibility seriously. You're the only one who didn't. If there aren't consequences, nobody will think that's fair."

She paused.

"You'll take the entire remaining fire watch tonight. Consider it a lesson for everyone."

Maggie had no choice.

She wasn't about to challenge the group's leader.

Quietly, she nodded and wrapped her blanket tighter around herself before sitting beside the fire.

The others seemed satisfied with the punishment.

Soon enough, snores filled the darkness once again.

Maggie sat alone, exhausted and miserable, feeding logs into the flames.

Mrs. Harris.

A nobody.

An uneducated old woman.

And somehow she thought she was qualified to lead everybody?

Just wait.

The moment Maggie found an opportunity, she'd make sure the old woman paid for this humiliation.

The next morning, breakfast preparations began at dawn.

Their supplies weren't sufficient for three proper meals a day.

Mrs. Harris used a small amount of rice to make several large pots of thin rice porridge.

Everyone drank a bowl.

It barely counted as food, but at least it warmed them up and provided a little energy.

After packing their belongings, the group set out once more.

...

Standing in the east parlour, Lila lowered her binoculars and stared toward the distant cluster of buildings.

Workers were raising walls around the developing settlement.

At this point, she had fully accepted that refugees would soon be living nearby.

"Well, whatever," she muttered. "Maybe life will be a little more interesting."

She slipped the binoculars back into the Vault and stopped paying attention to the construction.

The future refuge wasn't actually that far away.

If she cooked anything with a strong smell, the wind could easily carry the scent toward the settlement.

It would be smarter to prepare a large batch of aromatic food now and store it in the Vault.

That way she'd always have ready-made meals while minimizing any suspicious smells drifting outside.

She decided to start with something simple.

Corn.

Corn itself wasn't particularly rich or heavy, but when it boiled, its sweet fragrance traveled surprisingly far.

Lila filled a large iron pot halfway with water.

After washing the ears thoroughly, she dropped them in with a thin layer of husk still attached.

For extra sweetness, she sprinkled several spoonfuls of sugar into the pot.

Then she covered it and crouched beside the kitchen range to light the fire.

Before long, the water reached a rolling boil.

At first the aroma was faint.

Fifteen minutes later, sweet corn fragrance filled the entire house.

It smelled incredible.

Lila rarely remembered to eat corn.

Snowy and Patches had never tasted it before either.

Now both dogs sat nearby with half-closed eyes, noses lifted into the air, happily sniffing.

Their expressions made Lila laugh.

"You two are hopeless."

She chuckled.

"I'll let you try some later."

Today's batch was sweet sticky corn, which needed extra cooking time to become soft and tender.

She especially liked the kernels that burst open after simmering for a long time.

The longer it cooked, the stronger the aroma became.

Slowly, the scent drifted across the hillside.

It curled over a ridge and flowed down into the valley below.

A young soldier assembling insulated wall panels suddenly stopped and sniffed the air.

His hands slowed.

"You alright?" his partner asked.

The young man inhaled again.

"Do you smell that? Something sweet."

His partner put down his tools and straightened up.

Pushing back the fur-lined hood that covered most of his face, he looked toward the mountainside.

A solitary farmhouse stood there behind tall protective walls.

Red brick.

Dark roof tiles.

White smoke drifted lazily from its chimney.

The smoke swirled over the roof before the wind caught it and carried it downhill.

The older soldier stared for a moment.

Then he sighed.

"Someone's boiling corn."

The envy in his voice was unmistakable.

"Must be nice."

His face was hidden behind layers of winter gear and a thick mask.

The younger soldier couldn't see the wistful smile beneath.

"Being able to sit somewhere warm and cook fresh corn during the Long Freeze..."

He shook his head.

"That's living."

The words landed heavily.

Like a stone dropped into still water.

Around them, the workers who had been silently laboring suddenly became distracted.

One after another, people stopped what they were doing.

Seven or eight of them stood with hands on their hips, staring up toward the farmhouse on the mountain.
