Chapter 90

"My feet feel like they're freezing off."

"Can we rest a little?"

"I'm exhausted."

Complaints drifted through the group nonstop.

Breathing heavily, Mrs. Harris dropped her rope.

She unfolded the map and studied it.

Without measuring equipment, she had no way of knowing exactly how far they had traveled.

At least they were still heading in the right direction.

"Everyone tired?"

"Then let's rest here."

"There are trees nearby."

"Gather whatever wood you can find and we'll warm ourselves up."

Her voice came muffled through layers of scarf and cloth.

Half the group immediately sat down.

Nobody wanted to collect firewood.

They barely had enough energy to stand.

Mrs. Harris's expression hardened.

"You want to freeze to death out here?"

"We've only been walking for a little over an hour."

"We still have at least four more days ahead of us."

"If you're already giving up now, none of us are making it."

The sharp rebuke worked.

Grumbling quietly, people forced themselves back to their feet and headed into the nearby woods.

Before long, piles of branches and wood had been gathered inside an abandoned house nearby.

Everyone dragged their sleds and belongings indoors.

Doors and windows were shut tightly.

Twenty four pairs of eyes focused on the growing pile of firewood in the center of the room.

At that moment, nothing looked more beautiful.

Mrs. Harris pulled a box of matches from her coat pocket.

She lit a bundle of dry grass brought from the basement.

Soon the fire caught.

Orange and gold flames climbed steadily higher.

Warmth began spreading through the room.

The survivors crowded closer.

Some even removed their boots and socks, stretching their feet toward the fire.

After only a single hour outside, many feet had already turned a frightening shade of bluish purple from the cold.

"My feet are freezing. They hurt so bad."

"We've only been walking for an hour and we're already like this. Are we really going to make it to the refuge?"

The first waves of doubt began spreading through the group.

Just one hour into the journey, people were already thinking about turning back.

Today, Mrs. Harris was far stricter than usual.

The moment she heard the complaints, she shot the speaker a look sharp enough to cut steel.

"If you don't want to go, turn around and head back."

"But don't stand here poisoning everyone else's determination."

The complaints died immediately.

People fell silent and focused on warming their hands and feet by the fire.

Mrs. Harris glanced at the flames and felt it would be a waste not to use them properly.

She pulled two kettles from her sled, stepped outside, and packed them full of snow.

The kettles were placed directly over the fire.

Within fifteen minutes, both were boiling.

"Who wants hot water?"

After filling her own vacuum flasks first, she looked around at the group.

"Me."

"Over here."

"I'll take some."

Everyone quickly held up their containers.

The hot water they'd filled back in the basement had long since been consumed during the morning trek.

There were too many people.

Each person received only half a flask.

Lauren also collected half a flask of hot water.

She warmed her hands around it for a moment before opening the blankets covering her daughter's box.

"Here. Hold this. It'll keep you warm."

She passed the flask inside.

The tiny hand that appeared from beneath the blankets pushed it back instead.

"You keep it, Mom. I'm not cold."

Lauren smiled.

"I still have another one. And you're sitting right next to the fire."

She loosened the blankets slightly, allowing her daughter's face to peek out and enjoy some of the warmth from the flames.

Eventually the fire began shrinking.

A few people suggested gathering more wood, but Mrs. Harris stopped them.

"That's enough."

"We need to keep moving."

"We have to cover at least six hours of ground today."

Bracing her hands on her knees, she slowly stood and began preparing to leave.

"Can't we stay a little longer?"

"It's still warm in here."

"Leaving now would be such a waste."

Not everyone wanted to move.

Annabel's sharp voice echoed through the empty house.

"You always make people suffer. What's wrong with staying in a warm place a little longer?"

She lay on her sled wrapped in blankets.

After hours of freezing, she finally felt comfortable.

Mrs. Harris continued packing without looking up.

"Anyone who wants to stay can stay."

"Anyone who wants to follow me can follow me."

"But let me make one thing clear."

"If you keep arguing with every decision I make, you'll be staying behind on your own."

With that, she grabbed her rope and headed outside.

The others immediately scrambled to their feet.

Hats went back on.

Gloves were adjusted.

Sled ropes were lifted.

Nobody wanted to be abandoned.

Out here, falling behind meant death.

Under Edward's murderous glare, Annabel finally shut her mouth.

Maggie strained against the sled rope and hurried after the group.

 

They marched another two hours before finally reaching midday.

This time they stopped at another abandoned building.

As usual, everyone collected firewood, melted snow for water, and prepared lunch.

The morning's trek had drained nearly all their energy.

Mrs. Harris had brought a large iron cooking pot.

Several others had volunteered to carry additional pots.

Now every pot was set over a fire.

Everyone contributed a little food to the communal meal.

Snow was melted first.

Then potatoes, frozen cabbage, dried noodles, and rice were dumped into the boiling water.

A handful of salt followed.

A few random seasonings were added as well.

The mixture was stirred with a wooden paddle.

It became another batch of communal ration stew.

Before the Collapse, most people would have looked at the murky mixture and called it animal feed.

Now the sight of several steaming pots bubbling over open flames made mouths water.

The smell filled the room.

Cold.

Exhaustion.

Hunger.

All three combined into a single overwhelming anticipation.

When the food was finally ready, Mrs. Harris distributed portions just as she always had.

One ladle per person.

The stew was still scalding hot.

People blew on it and shoveled it into their mouths anyway.

Even when it burned their tongues, nobody wanted to waste a bite.

A hot meal at midday meant a better chance of avoiding dangerous heat loss later.

After lunch, Mrs. Harris allowed a thirty minute break.

Everyone crowded around the fire until their hands and feet regained some feeling.

Only then did they put their gloves and boots back on.

Half an hour later, the journey resumed.

 

They spent the entire day traveling through the city.

Whenever they stopped to rest or eat, abandoned buildings provided temporary shelter from the cold.

As evening approached, darkness slowly settled over the streets.

Mrs. Harris finally came to a stop.

Her feet and lower legs had gone almost completely numb.

She looked at an intact two story house that had somehow escaped the Lightning Storm.

"That's it for today."

"We'll spend the night here."

A wave of exhausted relief spread through the group.

"Thank goodness."

"Finally."

"I swear I'm about to die."

Callum felt as if the entire world had become warmer the moment those words left her mouth.

The front door was locked from the outside, which likely meant nobody was living inside.

Mrs. Harris recruited two stronger survivors.

Using large rocks, they smashed the lock.

The group filed inside.

There were no nearby trees for firewood.

Instead, they dismantled wooden furniture from inside the house and piled the pieces together.

Soon another fire crackled to life.

Warmth spread through the rooms once more.

It was the third fire of the day.

Each time flames filled a room, hope seemed to return with them.

Beyond the wood already burning, people searched neighboring rooms and gathered additional fuel.

The pile beside the fireplace grew steadily larger.

They intended to stay here overnight.

The fire would need constant feeding if they wanted any chance of staying warm.

Dinner looked much like lunch.

A mixture of ingredients simmered together with salt.

Everyone received another small bowl.

The hot stew drove away some of the cold and finally warmed their stomachs.

When people contributed food to the communal pots, Sophie, Leo, and Callum never revealed their instant noodles or other valuable supplies.

Some resources were simply too precious.

Even though everyone was traveling together, caution remained necessary.

It was better if nobody knew exactly what they were carrying.

After dinner, Mrs. Harris didn't even bother washing the cooking pots.

Instead, she filled them with snow and placed them back over the fire.

There was still a little grease and salt left inside.

Once melted, the water would become a thin broth.

Even that could help maintain strength and body heat.

The group gathered around the fire afterward.

Some people weren't wearing proper boots.

Snow had seeped inside throughout the day's march.

Now they removed their socks and wrung out the moisture.

Shoes and socks were arranged around the flames to dry.

Their exposed toes were mottled blue and purple from the cold.

Yet nobody seemed surprised.

Frostbite had been expected from the moment they chose to leave.

Callum and Leo were among the few exceptions.

Their reindeer hide boots reached nearly to their knees.

The thick fur lined footwear looked sturdy, warm, and practically indestructible.

As everyone sat around the fire, more than a few eyes drifted toward those boots.

The envy was impossible to hide.
