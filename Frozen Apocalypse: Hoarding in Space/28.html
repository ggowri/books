Chapter 27

Lila thought about the cotton gloves she had bought. They really would not be enough against extreme cold, so she nodded and agreed.

She scanned Callum's payment code and paid $24,000 in one go.

Callum looked at the money and grinned so widely he could hardly close his mouth. He even gave Lila the black leather bag.

Back at the hotel, Lila took out the hats and boots openly and examined them again.

The workmanship was excellent, and the materials were reassuringly solid. She was extremely satisfied.

On 25 June, with only two days left before the beginning of the Collapse, Lila drove the Luton van into the countryside.

She found a watermelon field.

A dark, thin old man was sitting under a rough grass shelter, weaving a willow basket. Sweat rolled down from his temples one drop at a time, but he showed no intention of stopping.

In the field, round watermelons lay under the blazing sun, their skins slightly warmed by the heat.

Lila stood at the roadside, crouched down, and patted one of the melons. The vibration was strong. It was fully ripe.

"How much are your watermelons?" Lila asked.

The old man, who had been twisting willow strips, looked surprised when he heard her voice. His watermelons were usually sold wholesale by the whole batch. Very few people came here to buy one or two.

If someone from the village came by, he usually just gave them one for free.

"If you want one to eat, take one. Wholesale price is fifty pence a pound."

The old man smiled and put down the willow in his hands.

Lila shaded her eyes from the fierce sunlight and looked towards the far end of the watermelon field.

It was a proper-sized patch.

"Are most of them ripe?"

"Most of them are."

The old man was not good at business. He simply answered whatever people asked.

"I'll take all the ripe watermelons in this field."

Since Lila had started stockpiling for the Collapse, the words she had said most often were, "I'll take it," and, "I'll take all of it."

The expressions she had seen most often on sellers' faces were shock and delight.

After confirming several times that Lila really did want everything, the old man called his son and daughter-in-law over.

They began picking the watermelons, weighing them and loading them.

It would start raining tomorrow, and Lila was afraid they might run out of time. While the family picked watermelons, she drove off to find a field of sweetcorn.

In the end, she bought 1.5 tonnes of sticky sweetcorn and one tonne of supersweet sweetcorn at $2 a pound.

Lila specifically told them not to strip off the outer husks of the sticky sweetcorn. They were to snap the cobs off with the husks still on, pack them into sacks, weigh them, and take payment.

Both sellers were busy out in the fields, so Lila simply gave them the address of Lockridge Storage Unit.

Rural families generally had small four-wheeled farm carts for transporting grain.

The two households each drove their own carts back and forth, delivering batch after batch to the storage unit.

That saved Lila the trouble of running around herself.

She only had to wait at the unit. As each batch arrived, she transferred it into the Vault.

It was almost dark by the time the whole watermelon field and several tonnes of sweetcorn were finally dealt with.

There were five tonnes of watermelons in total, and 2.5 tonnes of sweetcorn.

After paying, Lila had $291,000 left.

There was only one day to go before the 27th, yet she still had more than $200,000 in hand. Lila did not know whether to laugh or cry.

She had tried to save wherever she could, and now she had money left with nowhere obvious to spend it.

Tomorrow, she would do everything possible to clear out the remaining balance.

On 26 June, Lila woke early at the hotel and felt a chill around her shoulders.

Still half-asleep, she pulled open the curtains. Outside, the sky was terribly overcast. It was clearly only nine in the morning, yet it looked as though the sun had already set.

Lila went out at once.

She drove to five different chemists and stockpiled a large batch of medicine.

She bought another round of medicines for common illnesses, along with the necessary antibiotics.

At ten o'clock, Lila began weaving between different takeaway shops.

Five portions here, ten portions there. She kept going until two in the afternoon and stockpiled plenty of food from outside.

Spicy hotpot, marinated duck, rice noodles, burgers, pizza. She bought dozens of portions of each.

Although Lila would cook for herself after the Collapse, the flavour of restaurant food could not be replaced. In future, every outside meal she ate would be one fewer left in the world.

After that, she went to an ice cream wholesaler.

Orders of more than ten ice creams were twenty per cent off. Orders of more than fifty were thirty per cent off.

Lila bought them by the box. As long as it was a flavour she liked, she bought it in large quantities. In total, she moved fifty boxes.

After putting the ice cream into the Vault, she observed the weather.

The temperature outside was slowly dropping, though the rain was not yet heavy.

Lila could not see clearly from inside the van, so she got out, opened an umbrella, and checked the situation.

The rain was indeed light, no more than a fine drizzle.

Lila tightened her jacket around herself. It really was chilly.

Just then, she suddenly heard a faint cry near her feet.

Had the rainwater flooded the drains and washed out some enormous sewer rats?

Disgusted, Lila stepped aside and instinctively looked down.

On the ground, two mud-covered things were trembling faintly in a puddle.

"They really are giant rats."

The two muddy little bundles wriggled and cried at the same time, which genuinely turned Lila's stomach.

She was just about to give one of them a kick when she realised the head on the muddy bundle did not look right.

Rats had pointed heads.

These two things had round ones.

Lila turned on the torch on her phone and shone it over them.

Good grief.

They were two newborn puppies.

They were covered head to toe in mud and soaking in the puddle.

The two tiny mudballs were huddled together, shivering violently and letting out weak little whimpers now and then.

Without even frowning, Lila picked up the two small things, shook off some of the water, and placed them on the mat in the back seat of the van.

The Collapse was almost here.

Consider it a good deed.

She had saved two lives. Perhaps that would help her pray for her own survival through the Collapse.

She had originally planned to return to the hotel, but looking at the two little things huddled together and trembling on the seat cushion, Lila decided to make a trip to the pet shop.

By seven in the evening, Lila had followed the satnav to two pet shops, both of which were already closed.

She was not sure whether it was because it was too late or because there was no business in the rain.

Fortunately, the superstore was still open.

Lila swept into the store and went straight to the pet section.

Five large twenty-pound bags of dog food. Ten ten-pound bags of puppy food. Ten tubs of goat's milk powder.

She had no intention of buying enough food to feed these two dogs for the rest of their lives. They were still small, so dog food would do for the transition. If they were lucky enough to grow up, they would eat whatever Lila ate.

Between the pet section and the checkout area was the kitchenware section.

When Lila saw the casserole dishes and coarse clay jars, she could not move on.

The thought of brewing tea above her inglenook fireplace made it impossible to leave without buying a clay tea jar.

She bought three cream-coloured tea jars and two more casserole dishes.

Then she moved five crates of spirits, each costing $200.

Alcohol would be barter currency after the Collapse. The more, the better.

Seeing that she still had some money left, she bought five hundred kilos of the supermarket's highest-grade beef at $50 a pound, along with a large quantity of beef rolls and lamb rolls.
