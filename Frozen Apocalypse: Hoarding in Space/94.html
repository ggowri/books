Chapter 93

After two straight days of trekking through the brutal cold, the refugees had reached their limit.

The freezing temperatures were bad enough.

The physical exhaustion was even worse.

Many people were barely hanging on.

The moment they entered the farmhouse, they dropped their gear and slid down against the walls, too tired to care about anything else.

Back at Oakwood Estate, there had only been a handful of insulated survival suits available.

Mrs. Harris had kept one for herself and distributed the rest among the estate staff.

Looking at everyone now, it was obvious who had benefited.

The only people still in relatively decent condition were the former estate workers, Leo, Callum, and Sophie.

Once the bodies had been moved outside, Mrs. Harris took a look around.

The group needed heat immediately.

Fortunately, there was still plenty of wood indoors.

A wardrobe.

A storage cabinet.

Even a wooden kitchen cupboard.

"Come on," she called. "Let's start with the kitchen cabinet. It'll split easily."

She pulled an axe from a sled and led several volunteers toward the kitchen.

Leo and Callum were exhausted.

Even breathing felt like work.

Both had intended to collapse somewhere and rest.

But seeing Mrs. Harris and the others already chopping wood, they exchanged a glance.

Might as well help.

"Watch our stuff for a bit," Callum said to Sophie.

Sophie was sitting on her sled, completely drained.

"Fine by me."

The two men grabbed axes and joined the chopping crew.

The cabinet was old and brittle.

A few swings reduced it to pieces.

Several more chops turned the larger planks into usable firewood.

The farmhouse was cramped.

Fitting more than twenty people inside was already pushing it.

Fortunately, there was both a heated bed and a kitchen range.

Mrs. Harris decided to use the range as the primary heat source.

The large iron pot was left empty.

No water.

Just direct heat.

Flames roared beneath it.

Before long, the thick iron sides became scorching hot.

The kitchen warmed first.

Heat radiated from the iron pot and gradually spread throughout the room.

People resting in the adjoining rooms began drifting toward the kitchen.

Anywhere warmer was better.

Those who had recovered a little strength started helping.

"Once you're rested, split the wardrobe and the big cabinet too," Mrs. Harris instructed.

"Anyone not working can take over tending the fire tonight."

Nobody argued.

They were surviving as a group.

That meant everyone contributed.

After speaking, Mrs. Harris grabbed a hammer and smashed the lock off the large cabinet.

The lock broke instantly.

Inside sat a half-filled sack of millet.

Maybe five or six pounds.

Her eyes lit up.

"We've got food!"

The room erupted.

"We found millet!"

The excitement spread immediately.

Nobody could remember the last time they'd had a proper bowl of millet porridge.

Mrs. Harris searched the cabinet thoroughly.

No other grain remained.

But she did find a handful of goji berries.

Perfect.

Those could go into the porridge.

"Mrs. Harris!"

Someone warming themselves in the kitchen called out.

"Found a crock of pickled vegetables in the corner. It's frozen solid, though."

Mrs. Harris hurried over.

Inside were pickled cucumbers, peppers, spring onions, and other vegetables locked in a block of ice.

"That's excellent."

Her smile widened.

"We'll have them with the porridge tonight. Bring it closer to the stove and let it thaw."

Carrying the millet, she headed back toward the kitchen range.

The temperature inside continued to climb.

Warm air flowed from the kitchen into the rest of the house.

For the first time all day, the farmhouse no longer felt like an icebox.

Once the room had warmed enough, Mrs. Harris instructed several people to bring in buckets of snow.

They dumped them into the iron pot.

The snow hit the hot metal with a violent hiss.

White steam exploded upward.

Moments later, bubbles appeared beneath the snow as it rapidly melted.

When the water became warm, Mrs. Harris ladled some out and distributed small cups to everyone.

Even warm water felt luxurious.

More snow was brought in.

More melting followed.

After the second batch liquefied, she used some of the water to rinse the millet.

Then she poured the grain into the pot.

The lid went on.

The fire was fed.

Three pounds of millet simmered inside.

Enough to make an enormous pot of porridge.

Everyone had suffered today.

They'd found food.

Tonight deserved a small celebration.

"Just wait," Mrs. Harris announced. "You'll all have porridge soon."

Only then did she finally allow herself a moment to sit.

Her legs ached from calf to hip.

Every step today had felt like dragging weights through snow.

Thankfully, she had avoided serious frostbite.

Apart from the pain in her feet and lower legs, the rest of her body had survived intact.

At that moment, an argument broke out near the kitchen range.

Several people were fighting over the warmest spot.

"Come on, you've been sitting there forever!"

A man with bare legs squeezed forward aggressively.

His skin was covered in swollen red and purple frostbite sores.

The man already sitting by the stove snapped back.

"I'm not just warming myself. I'm tending the fire."

His own injuries were visible beneath torn layers of clothing.

"We can tend the fire too."

Someone else shoved forward.

"You don't get to hog all the good spots."

Mrs. Harris pressed a hand against her forehead.

The argument was giving her a headache.

"Enough."

Her voice cut through the room.

"Stop it. The whole house is warmer now."

She glared at them.

"If you've still got enough energy to fight over a stove, then clearly none of you are tired enough."

Silence followed immediately.

Since the journey had begun, everyone had relied on her judgment.

Her authority had only grown stronger.

When she spoke, people listened.

The argument ended on the spot.

The house was warmer.

But not warm enough.

Unless someone sat right beside the stove, there was no chance of removing boots and socks to properly warm frozen feet.

All twenty-plus sleds had been dragged inside.

Between the floor and the heated bed, nearly every inch of space was occupied.

People sat quietly on their sleds and waited.

The millet porridge simmered for over ten minutes.

Gradually, a rich grain aroma filled the room.

Mrs. Harris stirred the pot with a large ladle.

Then she pressed a hand against the heated bed.

The end nearest the firebox was already warm.

If the fire continued burning, the entire bench would become pleasantly hot.

Tonight, people might actually sleep well.

The bench was fairly large.

Packed tightly together, it could hold more than a dozen people.

The problem was simple.

Nobody wanted to sleep on the floor.

Mrs. Harris watched everyone squeezing toward the warmest section and came up with a solution.

She marked off roughly the hottest third of the hearth bench.

"People sleeping near the fire end have to stay sitting up."

She pointed farther down.

"Anyone farther away can lie down."

The room went quiet.

A few people looked ready to object.

Then they thought about it.

Actually...

That was fair.

The hottest spots came with a disadvantage.

The cooler spots came with the comfort of lying down.

Nobody could come up with a better arrangement.

The matter was settled.

Another half hour passed.

The porridge had thickened beautifully.

The smell alone was enough to drive everyone crazy.

Every pair of eyes in the room stared at the pot.

Some looked practically feral.

"Dinner!"

Mrs. Harris grabbed the giant ladle.

"Time to eat."

Today's reward was an enormous pot of thick millet porridge.

Everyone received a large bowl.

Unlike previous meals, there were actually plenty of grains in it.

Callum accepted his bowl with both hands.

After blowing on it, he took a cautious sip.

His eyes nearly watered.

"This is unbelievable."

He shook his head.

"I haven't felt this full in ages."

"It smells amazing."

Meanwhile, Mrs. Harris carried over the partially thawed crock of pickled vegetables.

Each person received one piece.

A slice of radish.

A cucumber.

A pepper.

Whatever ended up in the bowl.

No choosing.

No complaints.

Nobody cared.

After days of hunger and exhaustion, this was the closest thing they'd had to a feast in a very long time.
