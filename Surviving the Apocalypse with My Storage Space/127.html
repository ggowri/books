Chapter 127 The So-Called Truth

“She told me you were university classmates, that you shared a dormitory, that she was your closest friend. And with the other girl dead, I was worried something might happen to her, so I agreed to bring her over. I really did mean well. I just made things worse again.”

Clara looked miserable. “Evelyn, I’m sorry.”

“It’s fine.”

By the time they reached the Administration Hall, Evelyn felt a chill run through her at the sight of the queue.

So many people had bought the tins.

Life was hard now, and most people had been too reluctant to eat them straight away, which may well have saved them. But many of those tins had been given to children.

The base authorities were clearly terrified that people might discover the truth about the meat tins and revolt. At the same time, they could not just do nothing while the death toll kept climbing. So they had chosen a compromise: recall every tin, dispose of them, and wipe out the evidence altogether.

Evelyn and Adrian joined the queue. One tin could be exchanged for a small bowl of rice, or for compressed biscuits, vegetables, fruit, cold medicine, clothes, sanitary towels, or household goods.

More and more people kept arriving. There were five lines in total, and Evelyn and Adrian stood one behind the other.

The middle-aged man in front turned and looked Evelyn over. Then, quite deliberately, he stepped back and brushed her shoulder with his arm.

Evelyn had not even moved when Adrian kicked him clean off his feet.

“What’s going on here?” one of the patrol men barked, striding over.

The man was sprawled on the ground howling, but he still managed to strike first with his complaint.

“He attacked me for no reason. Officer, my stomach’s killing me. Arrest him.”

Adrian said nothing. He only looked at the man.

“Is that so?” Evelyn said coolly. “Did he really kick you for no reason? If you hadn’t been staring at me like that and trying to put your hands on me, would he have touched you at all?”

The man’s face flushed crimson. Adrian’s kick had been vicious enough that he still could not get up.

“What rubbish. You two are bullying an honest man. Officer, they’re lying. I never touched this woman. She’s wearing a mask, her hair’s cut that short, I thought she was a bloke. What do you take me for? Why would I harass a man?”

“Maybe you’re just that sort of deviant,” Evelyn said, her expression turning cold.

“If anyone here saw this man harassing someone, step forward and say so.”

The patrolman swept a glance over the waiting crowd, but the people in the queue looked numb and hollow-eyed. Not a single one came forward.

“Even if I were going to harass someone, it would be a proper little beauty with curves. A great lanky thing like you? I wouldn’t touch you if you paid me.”

As he spoke, he rubbed at his neck and kept right on sneering at Evelyn.

The patrolman told Evelyn to remove her mask so he could confirm whether she was male or female. Her voice was rather low, she was wrapped up from head to toe in drab clothes, and she was tall enough that even he was uncertain.

It was the first time Evelyn had ever run into something so absurd.

Still, not wanting to hold up the queue, she quietly removed her mask and pressed a hand over Adrian’s wrist to stop him doing anything rash.

The moment the patrolman and the man on the floor saw her face, both of them fell silent.

She had not bothered with any disguise that day. Her face was clean, and her already delicate features seemed even more striking for it.

“May I put my mask back on now?”

The patrolman nodded. Then he turned to the man. On charges of harassment and causing trouble, the man was dragged away.

Adrian reached over and pulled Evelyn’s mask so far up it nearly covered her eyes.

Evelyn turned to him and asked under her breath, “The needle?”

“I gave it to him.”

The corner of Evelyn’s mouth curved faintly. “Good.”

Calm returned to the queue quickly enough, and before long it was their turn to exchange their goods.

“What do you want?”

Evelyn glanced at a plastic basin behind the table, then set all the meat tins down in front of the clerk.

“I want basins. Or buckets.”

The man counted the tins. Thirty-five in all. In return, Evelyn was given two plastic buckets.

The queue behind them was still enormous. Some people had only one tin in hand. Others were carrying great sacks full of them.

Evelyn and Adrian were just about to leave when another disturbance broke out at the front.

“The meat tins were made from dead pigs. There are worms in them. Anyone who ate the tins was infected. The people in charge at the base have no conscience. They fed us rotten meat, killed tens of thousands of people, and dumped the dead in a huge pit east of the base. There is no Burial Grounds. It’s a mass grave. The rotting flesh seeped into the groundwater. The water in this base is corpse water.”

A man was shouting at the top of his lungs. The residents waiting in line heard him and surged towards him at once, stunned and desperate to know whether it was true.

Then came several deafening cracks.

The man was hit by multiple shots and collapsed into the crowd, still clutching a banner painted in blood with the words Justice and Truth.

“Was what he said true? Why would the base do this? Why are they trying to murder us?”

A woman rushed forward, seized one of the patrol men by the collar, and began screaming the questions into his face.

The news shattered what little reason people had left. They all surged to the front, desperate to storm inside and demand the truth.

“My daughter only ate one tin. It was her birthday, so I bought her one, and it killed her. I want every one of you to pay for my child.”

“My son was only three. He was only three.”

They had survived the natural disasters, only to fall victim to the base’s neglect.

The crowd went mad. They smashed and overturned things inside the building, shouting and sobbing. The patrol fired warning shots into the air, but it no longer made the slightest difference. Nothing could suppress the fury now.

Evelyn and Adrian stood at the very edge of the crowd, watching it unfold, feeling only anger and helplessness.

What was anyone supposed to do?

Not a single administrator had shown their face. They did not even possess the courage to stand before the public’s rage.

Evelyn had no idea how many people had already died in the nematode crisis, or how many more still would. The dead were not statistics. They were lives.

At first it had been the elderly.

Then the children.

Then the young.

Reinforcements from the patrol and response teams arrived carrying electric batons. Anyone who rushed forward was struck down on the spot, an ugly attempt to frighten the rest into submission.

“We should go.”

Adrian took the buckets from Evelyn and pulled her away by a side path. Even after they had gone a fair distance, she could still hear wave after wave of grief-stricken sobbing behind them.

“Evelyn, that was terrifying. Are you all right?”

Clara and Elliot came running after them. Clara glanced at the compressed biscuits in someone’s hands nearby, then looked back at Evelyn.

Evelyn shook her head. “We’re fine.”

“How did this suddenly come out?” Clara said, breathless. “This is a disaster. People are panicking, and they’re furious. I’ve no idea how the base is meant to contain this now. If anyone else dies, I think people will start leaving.”

For once Clara sounded entirely serious.

And she was right.

If the base could not resolve this within three days, a great many residents might leave.

“Evelyn, are you going?”

Even if the base announced that the water had now been purified, who would still dare stay? Who would still dare drink it?

Evelyn had the persistent feeling that worse disasters were still to come. Every instinct told her that, for the moment at least, staying at the base was the safer choice.

“We’re not leaving.”
