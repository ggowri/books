Chapter 48 Heatwave, Staying Home

High temperatures and water shortages had become the most severe problems. After the torrential rains and floods, the water system was wrecked, and both water pollution and air pollution got worse.

Once the June heat set in, the riverbed began to dry up. The Crystal River that cut through the center of Elaris went from overflowing, collapsing, and bursting its banks to drying out completely. Now it was nothing but scattered sandbars and muddy bogs. Bodies littered the dried river embankment. Riots could break out at any moment, all for the chance to snatch a handful of filthy water.

Calvin cut Evelyn a piece of cactus. He had brought it back while scavenging. The cactus had already shriveled and dried out, but he looked thrilled, like he'd found treasure. Evelyn accepted his kindness and, as a return gift, took out a pound of raw soil from her space. Calvin assumed it was leftover potting soil from her home, then he shared a bowl of polluted water he had fought for at the riverbank.

Evelyn carried the cactus back to her apartment. She immediately pulled out a flowerpot and soil and began transplanting it. Cactus was heat-resistant. When there was no water and no food, it could keep you alive. Elaris was already bare, not a blade of green left. The roadside trees had been chopped down during the storms by residents hauling them home for firewood. Otherwise, eating leaves or chewing bark could have been one more way to survive.

After a long period of hunger, the human body tended to end up in one of two states, wasting away until you were skeletal, or swelling from edema. And when you went without water and salt for too long, it was not just dizziness, shaking, weakness, and exhaustion. Teeth could loosen. Hair could start falling out.

Even so, plenty of people still forced themselves out into the heat to look for food. Calvin said there were also more and more snake-hunting teams outside now.

Staying indoors during the day and going out at night had become the standard routine for survivors. Nighttime temperatures stayed high, but without the brutal glare of the sun, you did not have to fear heatstroke or dropping dead from direct exposure.

The downside was getting robbed was far too easy. Even someone as big and strong as Calvin kept coming back covered in new injuries.

After three days of staying home, Calvin brought back news. The authorities had set up a base in Drakepool Town, thirty kilometers outside Elaris. Everyone who had gone to the shelters earlier had been transferred there. Supposedly the base was huge, big enough for a million people. The military controlled it, the rules were strict, and personal safety could be guaranteed.

As long as you brought your ID, you did not need to hand over any supplies. You could enter Drakepool Base.

Calvin was testing her, trying to see what she thought. Without realizing it, he no longer treated Evelyn like a nineteen-year-old girl. He saw her as an adult, sharp-minded, decisive, with both courage and strategy.

Evelyn was not going to the base. But going there at least meant you would not starve, and for most people, that made it the best option.

In her last life, Evelyn had not gone to the base either. Still, she knew quite a bit about it.

It was built by the authorities and controlled by the military, but power struggles still ran through it. Old elite families banded together to compete for control, forming a three-way standoff with the authorities and the military, each side checking the others.

The wealthy and powerful, even at the end of the world, still held the best resources in their hands.

That was only the negative side. Everything had two faces. From the positive side, the base was at least a safe refuge, and as long as you were willing to work, you could survive there.

After entering the base, if you followed the rules and did the jobs assigned to you, you could receive food and a bed. You could also get clothing and medicine.

"I'm not going," Evelyn said. She didn't hide it from him.

After hearing her answer, Calvin felt like he'd made up his mind.

The base was a miniature society. Even with strict controls, it was still a chaotic mix of all kinds of people. If he went with Wendy, it could bring even bigger risks and trouble.

"Wendy and I aren't going either," he said. "I heard they manage men and women separately there. If Wendy gets separated from me, I can't even imagine what would happen."

Ever since she'd been frightened, Wendy had grown withdrawn. It was only temporary muteness, but with her condition, she really wasn't suited for the base.

Two days later, after Calvin came back from fetching water, he told Evelyn that a lot of people had left over the past few days. The three women from Block E had gone to the base too. They dumped the two kids behind like useless baggage.

That night, Evelyn saw Calvin take the two kids out to fetch water. They didn't limp back until just before dawn.

Evelyn hadn't gone out in days. The cactus transplant had taken, and she didn't expect to be good at growing things. If she ever got the chance, she could find the right place, fence off a plot of land, build a house, grow fruits and vegetables, raise a few chickens, ducks, and geese. Living off the land like that did not sound bad.

Two days later, the two kids from Block E moved into Block D, on the twelfth floor.

Through Calvin, Evelyn learned their names and ages.

Ray was ten, while Ian was eleven.

How they'd ended up in those people's hands, what they'd been through, none of that mattered anymore.

Evelyn didn't oppose what Calvin was doing, and she didn't exactly support it either. Most of the time, she stayed inside. She rarely ran into the boys. They were well-behaved, too. When they passed her door going up or down the stairs, they even made a point of stepping lightly.

The temperature climbed to forty-nine degrees Celsius. Even at night, going outside was impossible. The hot wind against your skin felt like a burn. People who set out for the base would collapse from exhaustion, pass out from heatstroke within half an hour or an hour. Once they hit the ground, they never got back up.

The white sky had changed color. Vast sheets of red cloud drifted and gathered, and the red-black sky looked like it was about to start dripping blood.

Evelyn stood on her balcony, ice water in her hand, but it couldn't press down the agitation twisting in her chest. A little snake poked its head out below. Within seconds, the scorching ground seared it to death.

Every evening, Ray and Ian went out to collect snakes that had died from the heat. They didn't dare leave the residential complex, so they searched between the buildings.

To keep the snake meat from spoiling, they dried it into jerky as soon as they brought it back.

By mid-August, two fever blisters had erupted on Evelyn's lips. She hadn't eaten meat in half a month, living on plain boiled cabbage and water. Somehow she still got overheated and inflamed.

She brewed a pot of cooling herbal tea and drank it for two days. The heat didn't go down. Instead, she started getting nosebleeds.

Everyone else was suffering too. Heatstroke, nosebleeds, fever blisters, vomiting, convulsions, icy hands and feet, and mild hypothermia. The symptoms rotated through the building like a curse.

There were only five people left in the building now. Evelyn couldn't just watch them die. By the end of the day, her hands were trembling from giving so many acupuncture treatments.

Thankfully, they all made it through. That near-death feeling was unbearable. It was like something was grabbing your soul and trying to rip it out of your body by force. You fought it like a tug-of-war, and if you lost, it meant total death.

Spontaneous fires caused by the extreme heat were happening more and more often. In the distance, high-rises churned with thick smoke. The stinging smell of burning carried across several streets. After a chemical furnace exploded at a factory, black smoke billowed into the air, and the sky turned gray and murky again.
