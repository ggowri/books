Chapter 136 Left Behind

The acid rain had stopped, but the black sludge coating the ground had not dried yet. It still gave off heat and thick fumes, and every so often it burst into flame without warning. No one dared go downstairs. People only stepped out of their flats and stared at the smoke-wreathed base beneath the blackened sky. Some wept. Others laughed with the wild relief of people who had survived by sheer luck.

A day later, a few finally ventured down to test the ground. The sludge still clung to their shoes, but it could be walked on. At once, people poured out of the apartment blocks and rushed towards the Settlement Market, the Underground Quarter, and the Administration Hall.

Calvin and the others were heading to the market to see whether any food remained. Evelyn and Adrian went with them.

Residents smashed through the market doors and forced their way into the shopfront villas. The moment they saw food, they stuffed it straight into their mouths. Others grabbed sacks and bed sheets, bundling up whatever they could carry.

People broke into the Underground Quarter as well, ready to loot everything in sight. The base security arrived soon enough, armed with electric batons and guns, and before long the residents were fighting them outright.

Calvin and his group managed to pick up quite a few leftovers in the chaos. Evelyn and Adrian were more interested in seeing how badly the base had been damaged by the acid rain, but after barely ten minutes outside, the sour, corrosive stink in the air was enough to make them dizzy.

Children and frail women were the first to suffer.

Some collapsed where they stood, foam gathering at their mouths. Others started bleeding from the nose, their bodies twitching violently.

Evelyn caught Adrian by the wrist and pulled him back out of the crowd. Once again, survival had become a matter of brutal selection. Those who could endure the poison left by the acid rain would live. Those who could not would simply become another sacrifice to this disaster.

The vegetables in the Underground Quarter were stripped in moments. Even the guards waving guns and firing warning shots could not stop the stampede. People yanked the vegetables from the soil and ate them on the spot. Even when they were shoved to the ground, they still clung to the food in their hands.

With the base cut off from electricity, some people tore down the wooden shelving in the market and carried it home for firewood. While scavenging, Evelyn spotted Sophie in the middle of a crowd, fighting over a packet of instant noodles. Evelyn bent down, picked up a cabbage someone had stepped on, and looked away.

Everyone was carrying pent-up rage after the acid rain. Some used the scramble for supplies as an excuse to vent it. They shoved people over and trampled them underfoot. Others managed to grab something, only to have it snatched away a second later.

The entire base had descended into chaos.

It could no longer be controlled.

Professor Whitaker and Secretary Bennett stood in a room that still smelt faintly of damp plaster and smoke.

“You mean that after the acid rain, all the water has been contaminated and can no longer be used?”

“Not only the water,” Professor Whitaker said. “The soil as well. Any ground touched by the acid has become wasteland. It has completely lost its ability to support crops. Secretary Bennett, there are still hundreds of thousands of people in this base. If they want to survive, I suspect the only option is to leave and search for a new place to live.”

Professor Whitaker had been the expert who developed the fast-growing wheat strain harvested in March. Before all this, he had been a professor at the University of Elaris, and a botanist besides. He also knew a great deal about geology and climate. Secretary Bennett trusted his judgement completely.

“Professor Whitaker, do you really think we can still find somewhere suitable? In just over a year, we turned this place from a small town into a base that could hold a million people. Are we truly meant to abandon it?”

The professor let out a weary sigh. “If you do not believe me, have someone dig ten metres, twenty, even thirty. You will not find healthy soil. As for fifty metres, or a hundred, I cannot say.”

Secretary Bennett had no choice but to issue the order. Every excavator in the base that still worked was sent out to dig, to test both the soil and the water table.

But just as Professor Whitaker had said, even below thirty metres, they found nothing usable.

“The acid cut through the earth quickly, and deeply. A single drop can ruin a vat of water. And it rained for ten days.”

Director Collins dragged a hand through his hair in frustration. “Then where exactly are we meant to go? The signal towers are gone, the poles are down, the power lines are wrecked. None of us can predict the future. How are we supposed to find somewhere with clean water and intact soil?”

“Perhaps northwest,” Professor Whitaker said. “The black sludge on the surface has already dried. Leaving now would not be especially difficult.”

He looked as though he meant to take his leave, but Secretary Bennett hurriedly stopped him.

“You are not coming with us?”

Professor Whitaker shook his head. “I am too old for the journey. I will stay here.”

“If you stay, how can the rest of us leave? The base may look full, but in truth there is scarcely anyone we can rely on. Every survivor here has been battered by one loss or another. I do not even know whether they would be willing to leave. And even if we reach the Northwest, what then? What if there is no clean water there either, no good land left at all?”

Professor Whitaker looked at him calmly. “If you can find the person who warned everyone, in the middle of the night, that acid rain was coming, perhaps there may still be a way.”

Secretary Bennett and Director Collins exchanged a glance.

Then both fell silent.

Finding one person in all this was like trying to pull a single grain of sand from the sea.

The chaos in the base never let up. Calvin and the others dug up some soil and carried it back, intending to grow vegetables indoors. Once the other residents saw what they were doing, they copied them at once. Now people were not only stealing supplies. They were fighting over soil as well.

But the most pressing problem remained water.

The leadership still had emergency reserves, but they refused to release them. The residents did not know where the water was stored, and the guards all carried weapons. They no longer bothered with warnings. If anyone came too close, they simply shot.

“I saw people digging on the east side of the base,” Eddie said. “They’ve been at it for days. Not a handful of good soil, not a drop of water, even after digging dozens of metres. We’re finished.”

Calvin shot him an irritated look. “Stop saying that every five minutes. We’re not finished yet. There’s always a way through.”

Eddie shook his head. Over the past two days he had been wandering the base, gathering whatever scraps of news he could, and he had already seen helicopters coming and going several times.

The leadership, he thought, were probably preparing to flee.

They would leave hundreds of thousands of survivors here to fend for themselves.

Three days later, at midnight, hundreds of lorries suddenly rolled out of the base.

They never came back.

When residents rushed to the Administration Hall to see what was happening, they found it completely emptied out. The Manor District, the luxury apartment blocks, the market, the Underground Quarter, everything had been stripped clean. Every member of the leadership council was gone. Every security guard had gone with them.

They had taken the glass from the buildings, the doors, the tables and chairs, the lightbulbs, the wiring.

They had taken everything.

Hundreds of thousands of survivors had been abandoned in the base. Some hurriedly packed their things and followed the tracks left by the tyres. Others, unable to bear the shock of being cast aside, chose to end their own lives under the strain of hunger, thirst, and the poison in the air.

Arson, murder, and looting became commonplace. Everyone still left in the base lived in fear, and before long people began escaping in groups under cover of darkness.

“Before the lorries left, every helicopter flew out as well,” someone said. “There’s no food in the base now. No water either. They did not even leave behind a hoe or a single packet of seeds. They took all the wheat from the fields.”

Even Evelyn had not expected the leadership to leave so suddenly, or to leave absolutely nothing behind.

They had taken all the highly skilled people, all the supplies, all the food.

What they left behind were the old, the weak, the sick, the injured, and an abandoned base already half dead.
