Chapter 133 The Acid Rain Arrives

At midnight, Adrian led Evelyn into Administration Hall. He switched off the main power, rendering the cameras useless. Evelyn walked confidently into the broadcast room. Once inside, Adrian destroyed the cameras and then restored the power.

Clearing her throat, Evelyn turned on the microphone.

“Attention, attention. All residents, please be advised: over the next few days, corrosive acid rain will fall. Do not leave your homes and ensure you store enough drinking water.”

Her voice carried a haunting gravity, leaving no room for argument. She kept the announcement brief and cut the broadcast. As soon as it ended, the power was cut again, and she and Adrian quickly left Administration Hall.

The broadcast had awakened the entire base. The words “corrosive acid rain” weighed heavily on everyone’s mind.

When the staff arrived at Administration Hall, the building was empty. All cameras had been destroyed, and the power was off.

The administrators wanted to dismiss it as a prank, but Secretary Bennett issued an urgent notice.

All residents were ordered to work in the wheat fields outside the base. In two days, every stalk of wheat had to be harvested.

The base began storing water overnight, filling every barrel and tank, even digging a few reservoirs. It was a tacit admission that the acid rain was coming. Yet, the person responsible for issuing the warning remained unknown, and management opposed Secretary Bennett’s unilateral actions.

“This is pure scaremongering,” one official argued. “Acid rain? Never heard of it. Should we also expect knives falling from the sky? The wheat isn’t ripe. Harvesting now will cause serious losses. Secretary Bennett, are you willing to gamble?”

Secretary Bennett remained calm. “It’s an order from above,” she said. No one else spoke.

The next day, the base’s broadcast rang out again.

“Emergency notice. All residents, stop your work and proceed to the wheat fields immediately. Each participant in the harvest will receive three pounds of wheat per day.”

The announcement left the residents stunned.

But three pounds of wheat was enough incentive. Most immediately headed to the fields, while some hesitated, afraid the acid rain might strike.

“I’m not going out. What if the acid rain comes suddenly?”

“If the base is sending everyone out, it means it’s safe. They wouldn’t risk so many people. I’ll go. Three pounds of wheat will last a long time.”

Evelyn and Adrian stayed inside. Wendy was brought to Evelyn. Officer Shaw and his team went to the fields. Evelyn tried persuading them not to go, but they couldn’t resist the reward.

She kept her eyes on the sky, uneasy. For some reason, she hoped the acid rain would be delayed by two days—until the wheat was harvested and the workers returned.

These people had finally dared to hope for life again. She silently prayed they would be spared.

From six in the morning until eight in the evening, the residents returned. No rain fell. Evelyn felt some relief.

Officer Shaw reminded her that tomorrow would be the final day of harvest. Those staying late in the fields would receive an extra three pounds of wheat.

Looking down, Evelyn saw elderly residents returning in groups. Some had lost all their family. Others had been cast out as burdens. They clung to each other, surviving by a thread within the base.

Children, five to eight years old, orphaned, had no choice but to work to feed themselves.

The base wasn’t a charity. Only labour earned food.

“The clouds tonight look wrong. Don’t go out tomorrow,” Evelyn warned again.

Eddie noticed her concern and spoke first.

“Alright. I won’t go. Last night’s broadcast must have come from the justice envoy. I trust them, and I trust you, Evelyn. Be careful, Shaw. Better safe than sorry.”

Officer Shaw agreed. If anything happened to him, what would happen to Wendy? He couldn’t take the risk.

The next day, far fewer residents went to the fields. Evelyn stood at the window, watching the passing hours.

By four in the afternoon, those harvesting wheat returned, smiling, carrying the base-issued grain. The harvest was complete ahead of schedule.

At four-thirty, the sun’s halo grew larger, and within minutes, a drop of black rain fell, striking the stainless steel clothes rack on the balcony with a metallic clang.

“Acid rain has arrived,” Evelyn whispered.

She looked at Adrian, eyes red and brimming with tears.

“Luckily, everyone’s back. Luckily, the wheat is harvested,” she said before collapsing to the floor, crying. She had been terrified—fearful that history would repeat itself like the parasite crisis, with tens of thousands dying.

But this time, they had acted in time.

Still, screams of pain rose from below. Some had already been touched by the acid rain.

“Run to the nearby building! Hurry!” someone shouted from the stairwell, tossing clothes and umbrellas down.

But the victims struggled only briefly before collapsing. No amount of shouting could save them.

Trees and grass turned black, corroded and scorched. Those lying in the rain slowly became white bones.

Electrical wires sparked and snapped.

A sudden “crack” plunged the building into darkness.

Outside, the sky darkened. The acid rain fell relentlessly, drumming on the glass and weighing on every heart.

Adrian knelt beside Evelyn, wiping her tears calmly.

“You’re amazing,” he said.

“I cried because I realised something,” she replied. “I still have blood in my veins. I can pick up a knife to protect myself, but I can also save others.”

But that was only possible if she could get out safely. Adrian was her escape. Without him, even if she detected the acid rain in advance, she wouldn’t have warned everyone. She couldn’t survive it alone.

“Thank you, Adrian.”

“For what?”

“For being so incredible,” she said with a small laugh.

After the acid rain began, all soil, water, and air would be corroded and polluted. Life would only become harsher.

Officer Shaw and his team had stored plenty of water. Everyone seemed to revert to the harsh days of extreme heat back in Elaris.

The next day, the acid rain continued without pause. The drains clogged. The black rain resembled oil, bubbling. The iron gate on the ground floor had already corroded into useless scrap.

Residents on lower floors began moving upwards. Everyone had experience now.

No one knew how long the acid rain would last. They quietly watched outside, expressions numb yet composed.
