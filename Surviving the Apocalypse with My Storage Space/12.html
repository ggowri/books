Chapter 12 Rainstorm: Part 8

At 2 a.m., the temperature plummeted to minus two degrees Celsius. Evelyn woke up from the cold, shivering. She pulled a backup battery from her storage space and laid an electric blanket on the bed. She'd thought she wouldn't need these things until the rain finally stopped. But once again, she had underestimated how brutal the weather would get.

Once the blanket started to warm up, Evelyn drank a cup of hot water, then crawled back into bed and wrapped herself tightly in the covers.

Her phone no longer had any signal at all. She stared at the soft glow of the emergency lamp on her nightstand, her mind blank.

Today was only the tenth day since the rain began.

*****

The next morning, Evelyn finally allowed herself to sleep in. At 10 a.m., a deafening roar filled the air above the complex. The sound of helicopter rotors echoed through the sky.

Evelyn jolted upright in bed. She held her breath, listening again.

It was real. A helicopter. The rescue teams were finally here, dropping supplies.

By 10:30, everyone in Block D had gathered on the twelfth floor. That floor had a secure door that led directly to the rooftop. The only problem was, the one and only key to that door was kept in Unit 1201. The man who lived there was a police officer, and people generally trusted him.

But when they got there, they found out the officer, Calvin Shaw, had only been back once since the flooding began. The key was still with him. And now, no one could get the door open.

"We have to break it down," David said. "If we don't go up, the rescue team will think the building's abandoned and won't drop anything."

"It's the only way," someone else agreed. "A few of us guys can kick it down. Shouldn't take more than a few hits."

The group quickly came to a decision. Excited and desperate, several men lined up and started kicking the door.

Evelyn and Claire stood together in the crowd, bundled tightly in layers, only their eyes visible.

Off to the right, Pauline stood stiffly. When she saw Evelyn, her face twisted with resentment.

Evelyn ignored her. Last night, she'd overheard Pauline's son throwing a tantrum for hours, crying about snacks and toy cars.

Most of the neighbors were fed up with the Rivers family already. Resources were tight, and every extra person meant fewer supplies to go around. Pauline had dragged her husband and that brat back here just in time to divide up life-saving goods. No one had any patience left for them.

If the Rivers family had any sense, they'd know it was time to keep their heads down and stay quiet.

"Stop kicking. I'm back."

A worn figure emerged from the bottom of the stairwell.

For a second, no one recognized him.

It was Calvin Shaw—the tall, clean-cut man who had once seemed so put together. Now, his clothes were torn and filthy, his face scruffy with a beard, and he looked like he'd aged ten years.

His parents and wife burst into tears the moment they saw him.

He gave them a few comforting words, then made his way to the front and pulled the key from his pocket.

Evelyn noticed faint bloodstains on the gray shirt beneath his tattered raincoat.

"Calvin, what happened to you?" someone asked. "And do you have any news? When will the power be restored? When will the rain stop? Why don't we have any signal? Did the cell towers go down?"

Calvin shook his head. "I don't know anything."

He unlocked the rooftop door, and the crowd surged up the stairs.

Thanks to the rooftop drainage system, there wasn't much standing water. The helicopter hovered above their building within minutes, dropping supplies swiftly. In less than thirty seconds, it was off to the next rooftop.

Several men hurried to haul the crates down to the twelfth-floor stairwell. Calvin locked the rooftop door again, then took his two-year-old daughter from his wife's arms.

"What happened?" she asked.

"Got ambushed by looters on the way back," Calvin said quietly.

"Are you going back to the station?" his wife asked.

"No. Things are getting out of control. I can't leave you all here alone."

Evelyn watched them speak in low voices. After a while, she noticed that Jessica Shaw's tense expression had eased a little.

Claire nudged her arm and leaned in to whisper, "There's barely anything."

Evelyn looked over at the supplies. One crate of bottled water. One crate of compressed biscuits. One crate of canned meat.

"We can't divide it by headcount," someone argued. "We should go by household. Some families only have one person. Others have eight or nine."

"That's even worse," someone else said. "So one person gets the same amount as eight? How is that fair?"

"If we're talking fair, then how about this—we divide based on property ownership. If your name's on the deed, you get a share. If not, you don't. Fair enough?"

No matter how they sliced it, someone was always going to feel cheated.

In the end, everyone turned to Calvin for a decision.

"Here's how we'll do it," he said. "Each household gets one bottle of water, three biscuits, and three cans of meat. That way, everyone gets something."

The Rivers family was still unhappy. They had eight people, the biggest household in the building. But since it was Calvin who made the call, they had no choice but to swallow their complaints.

Evelyn took her share and quietly returned home.

Three cans of braised pork, a small bottle of water, and the biscuits—dense and dry but surprisingly heavy. They didn't taste great, but once soaked in water, they expanded and could fill you up.

She grabbed two buns and ate lunch with one of the meat cans.

She'd planned to take a nap afterward, but just as she finished eating, someone knocked. To her surprise, it was Calvin.

"Evie, my parents aren't feeling well. Could you take a look? I've heard from the neighbors you're quite skilled."

Evelyn studied him carefully. And she knew that he was studying her too.

She was a nineteen-year-old prodigy. Abandoned by her biological parents at birth. Taken in and raised by Dr. Susan Carter on the tenth floor. Top of the science track. Accepted into university at sixteen. Her adoptive mother passed away when she was seventeen, and her adoptive father died just a few months ago. Now, she was all alone.

"I can take a look," Evelyn said evenly. "But I'm just average. If they need acupuncture, I'll need some firewood or supplies in return."

She was cautious with Calvin Shaw. He wasn't an ordinary person, and she couldn't afford to be careless.

He nodded. "No problem."

Evelyn went back to get her medical kit, which included a set of silver needles and surgical tools. She followed Calvin to his apartment and saw his parents. One glance was enough to tell what was wrong.

"Last night, the temperature dropped below freezing," Evelyn explained. "They're suffering from hypothermia. Thankfully, it's only moderate. They're shivering, weak, unable to speak clearly, with bluish lips and pale faces. I'll check their body temperature first. If it's too low, they'll need hospital care. Hypothermia can lead to heart and lung failure."

Calvin's parents weren't even sixty. If they'd been in their seventies or older, this could have turned fatal fast.

Severe hypothermia could cause unconsciousness and put the body into a kind of suspended state—like hibernation.

"Evie, is hypothermia really that dangerous?" Jessica asked, worried.

Evelyn nodded. "There are plenty of cases reported in the news. Marathon runners, hikers—if they get lost in cold weather and their body temp drops, it often ends in death."

"Thirty-four degrees," she said, checking the thermometer and handing it to Calvin and his wife.

"It's all my fault," Jessica said, her voice breaking. "I didn't notice how cold it got last night. It wasn't until Wendy started crying that I even woke up. I rushed into my parents' room, and they seemed okay at the time. If I'd checked their temperature then, we wouldn't have waited this long to realize what was happening."
