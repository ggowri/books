Chapter 5 Rainstorm

For reasons she couldn't explain, the closer the day came, the calmer Evelyn felt.

She felt no panic, fear, excitement, or grief.

On April 1, the apocalypse arrived.

Evelyn turned off her phone and lay in bed. She had already locked every window and door, bolted everything from the inside. This time, she wasn't going to participate in the chaos. She wouldn't join the screaming, the scrambling, the disbelief. She was done with that.

The next morning, at 8 a.m., the sun never rose.

The sky was stained a deep, unnatural red. People stepped out of their homes to start their day as usual, unaware that the world had already begun to unravel.

And then it hit.

Torrential rain poured from above, mixed with hailstones the size of fists. The sky darkened completely. Winds howled like beasts. Lightning split the sky open with a roar, thunder shaking the earth as if the heavens had cracked. Shrill howls and a strange, low sobbing seemed to echo through the wind.

At noon, Evelyn finally got up.

She didn't open the curtains. After a quick wash, she went straight into the kitchen and resumed cooking, as if it were just another day.

She had no idea what the world outside had become. She didn't want to know. She didn't dare to know.

The three-bedroom apartment felt strangely empty now. The living room was bare, save for a single sofa and a low coffee table.

Evelyn took a small family photo, slipped it into a locket, and hung it around her neck. Everything else had been packed away.

Her bedroom had only one bed left. The kitchen, however, remained untouched—fully equipped and ready.

Footsteps echoed now and then from the stairwell outside. Sometimes, she heard doors slamming hard next door. The tension was rising.

Later that afternoon, she cracked the curtain just enough to peek outside. She saw cars rushing in and out of the complex. People were soaked and frantic, sprinting for cover. Her eyes darkened slightly, then she drew the curtains closed again.

When she turned her phone back on, social media was exploding.

Everyone was talking about this once-in-a-century "superstorm." Some were angry. Some mocked the panic. Some posted videos or warnings. Some simply cursed the weather.

So many voices, so many reactions—and yet Evelyn felt absolutely nothing.

She kept scrolling and noted the timestamp. The storm had started at 8 a.m.

That was different.

The disaster itself was the same, but the timing had changed. In her past life, the rain had begun at 5 a.m. There hadn't been hail either. It had rained continuously from April 1 at 5 a.m. until June 1 at 5 a.m.

Now, people were starting to talk about that post—the anonymous warning from weeks ago. Some were asking: If the rain had started, how long would it last?

Evelyn couldn't answer that.

No one could.

She headed back to the kitchen and continued to cook, blocking out the outside world once more.

Every sewage outlet in her home had been sealed with cement. There was no backflow or flooding.

As for rescue operations, there was no guarantee whether they'd come or when. Would aid supplies be distributed? If so, when? No one knew. Nothing was certain anymore.

At this stage, global systems were still holding. Electricity and water were still running. People were complaining, but no one had crossed that invisible line yet. The truly terrifying thoughts hadn't emerged.

That night, floodwater began to rise around the base of the building. Online, countless cries for help were posted. But within minutes, they were drowned out—overwhelmed by a tidal wave of desperate posts, lost in the noise.

Evelyn heard screaming and cries for help from downstairs. It sounded like someone had been electrocuted.

She didn't go out.

The next morning, the hail had finally stopped.

But that only meant one thing: water and power would be cut off soon.

Thankfully, her tanks were full. She had prepared in time.

Sure enough, by that afternoon, the electricity and water shut down. Her phone signal began to weaken. Right then, she was added to a group chat by one of the building managers.

The name of the chat caught her eye: Willow Heights Residents Group 3. It was a group chat formed for mutual aid.

Evelyn raised an eyebrow but didn't leave the group.

There were 305 people in it—and more were being added every minute.

Evelyn lived in Block D of the residential complex.

The group chat was buzzing:

A302: Oh my god, that warning came true. What do we do now? When is someone coming to rescue us? I'm on the third floor and it's terrifying. The water's almost reached the first floor—and we've lost power and water. I'm freaking out.

C505: Didn't you see the news notifications? This is a global superstorm, not just Elaris. Rescue? We'll probably be waiting a while.

F601: How much did you all stock up? I only have two cabbages left at home.

A204: Anyone kind enough to take me in? I'll pay handsomely.

F601: You'd be better off booking a room at a high-rise hotel. They even feed you.

...

The chat suddenly went silent. Evelyn skimmed the messages, then muted the notifications.

Moments later, the signal cut out completely. No more messages came through. Half an hour passed, and she heard voices outside. Peeking out through the curtain, she spotted two middle-aged men in raincoats and life vests paddling a kayak out of the complex.

At 2 p.m., the signal returned. The group chat lit up again, this time with people discussing scavenging runs.

C702: What should I do? My husband hasn't come back. It's been five hours.

A203: That was your husband who went out? What was he thinking, going out in this kind of weather?

C702: We've got a baby. We ran out of formula and diapers.

C701: Mine went out too. We've got an elderly parent at home who's sick. He went to get medicine.

B404: Wait... you guys actually have a kayak? That's wild.

C702: Bought it for camping, of all things. Never thought it'd come in handy like this.

C401: I just cracked open the door—the water's even higher now. And the rain's getting worse. Do you think they might...

C701: Rachel Hayes, what are you trying to say? You better not be cursing my husband!

C401 replied with a smirking emoji.

C701 instantly snapped back with a string of insults. The two began arguing fiercely, only stopping when the group admin stepped in to shut it down.

After that, the chat fell into another tense silence. A few people tried to break the mood with comforting words, but no one dared to volunteer to help look for the missing men.

Fortunately, around 3:30 p.m., both men returned with a pile of supplies.

The mood in the group chat immediately lightened. Relief turned into celebration.

The fact that Block C had a kayak became a source of admiration. Everyone started complimenting them, hoping to borrow it for future trips.

Around 5 p.m., Evelyn heard shouting and crying coming from the balcony of David Rivers' unit next door. From the sound of it, water had flooded in through the balcony, likely overflowing from backed-up drainage.

Outside, the rain kept hammering the city. Thunder cracked the sky again and again. One of the nearby buildings had part of its rooftop blown clean off by lightning. The storm's winds were so fierce, trees in the complex were uprooted and hurled into the air.

Her phone kept lighting up with warning alerts: hailstorm, extreme weather, lightning, tornado, cold wave...

In some households, where too many people lived together and food supplies had run short, the rising water and delayed rescue efforts were pushing everyone closer to panic.

That night, just as Evelyn lay down to sleep, several urgent messages popped up in the chat.

Someone in Block E had been struck by lightning on their balcony. They were killed instantly. Ambulances and police couldn't be reached. The body had to be left in the home.

Building G, people said, housed over a dozen elderly retirees living alone. Most were over seventy.

By midnight, signal was spotty again. The temperature had dropped to just eight degrees. Evelyn pulled another thick quilt over herself and scrolled through the news for a long while, finally drifting off into a shallow sleep.

The next morning, the thunder and lightning had finally stopped.

More residents were venturing out, using the kayak and life vests borrowed from Block C. In small groups, they went to gather supplies.

Evelyn read through the group chat and learned most people were now looking for materials—glass to seal their balconies, cement to block their drains.

Last night, the floodwater had backed up through the drainage systems.

Every home had been flooded.
