Chapter 135 Soil Crisis

This was what the wasteland truly meant. The world had been ruined beyond recognition. The air, the water, the soil, all of it poisoned. Plants and animals had died out, habitats had collapsed, and the people left behind were forced to scrape out a life in the wreckage. There was no civilisation left, no morality, no clear line between right and wrong.

There was only survival.

“Adrian, do you think there’s any land left out there that hasn’t been touched by the acid rain?”

Adrian lifted his eyes to hers and gave a small nod. “There must be.”

“Really?”

“I believe there is.”

Curled beneath the blanket, Evelyn listened to the relentless patter outside and felt a despair deeper than anything she had known before.

What future could humanity possibly have once it had lost both clean water and usable soil?

The devastation was absolute. Poison filled the air. Would people begin to mutate? Would children born in the future come into the world deformed, broken, incomplete?

The next morning, when Evelyn got up and looked outside, the acid rain was still falling in a thin, steady curtain. Black water clung to the walls of the building, eating into them. Pip and Lark were huddled beneath the bed. Animals always sensed danger first, and the sight of them puffed up with fear was pitiful enough to break her heart.

Calvin and Eddie suited up and planned to go down to the ground floor to look for any flats the acid rain had not yet reached. Unfortunately, the corrosive water had already flooded as far as the corner staircase, so the two of them had no choice but to turn back.

“I reckon this rain will stop in ten days at most,” Calvin said. “Stop messing about. Getting splashed by that stuff is no small matter. Once it’s over, we can go down to the Underground Quarter and dig up some soil.”

Eddie let out a sigh. “That’s all we can do. We wrapped ourselves up so tightly just now I could hardly move, and even then a few drops landed on my clothes. Look at this. It burnt through the fabric almost instantly.”

If it had been skin, he did not even want to imagine the result.

Things were grim for everyone else in the building as well, though at least no one had died. It seemed the toxic haze was poisonous, but not immediately fatal.

This might have been an apartment block, but very few people still had proper food stores. Most were already hungry, and now there was no electricity and no running water either. Panic spread fast.

At least nearly everyone had managed to store some water. Without that, life would have been far worse.

Evelyn could also hear the cries of chickens, ducks, and geese carrying over from the newly built stockyard in the base. Those poor creatures had only just been bred and released, and now disaster had struck them all over again.

On the morning of the third day, the concrete below the building was finally eaten through. The pooled acid burned its way past the surface and into the soil, then seeped into the groundwater below.

Where the ground had been exposed, it looked like the surface of a honeycomb, riddled with holes and pitted everywhere.

Steam and thick vapour rose from the corroded earth, leaving behind a heavy layer of black sludge. The stench of acid spread through every corner of Blackwater Base.

Evelyn trained her binoculars on the outer wall of the building next door. The surface, eaten away by the black water, looked like stone weathered over centuries. It seemed likely it would crumble at the slightest touch.

The only thing that appeared able to withstand the acid was glass. A fallen pane downstairs had shattered, but aside from the break itself, it showed no sign of corrosion.

The neighbouring buildings had all begun to change. Their once clean outer walls had turned black under the rain. Corners of the top floors had been eaten through until they hung loose, and whenever the wind rose, chunks dropped away with a heavy crash.

By afternoon, a streak of violet lightning flashed across the sky. Thunder followed, and then the acid rain grew heavier and heavier. As the sky roared above them, it came down in sheets. White smoke hissed from the ground.

Evelyn checked the thermometer. The temperature was holding at thirty degrees and had not shifted at all.

Once the pipes were damaged, the bathroom had become unusable. Afraid the acid rain might backflow inside, Evelyn had filled the squat toilet with cement.

On the fourth day, people in Building Nine next door began to die. Crying, complaints, and furious shouting drifted from one flat to the next until everyone could hear it.

Some people had started robbing others under cover of darkness. One thief was shoved from an upper floor. He did not die from the fall. Instead, he landed in the black sludge below, which pinned him fast. Then the acid rain fell on him, and he was quickly corroded to death.

Ray and Evan were targeted too, but luckily Calvin spotted it in time.

The man behind it lived right next door to Calvin. He had once worked with the hunting crews. He lived with an elderly mother and a young son. When he was caught, he dropped to his knees and begged for forgiveness. His mother and child knelt beside him, crying and pleading with Calvin to spare him.

Calvin beat him soundly, then let him go.

As the days dragged on, tensions in the building grew worse. Robbery became almost routine. Someone discovered that Evelyn lived alone and tried to force her door, only to be caught in the act by Adrian.

Adrian was ruthless. There were still plenty of people in the building, and because he remembered what Evelyn had told him, he did not kill the intruder. Even so, the man was beaten badly enough.

After one or two such incidents, everyone in the building got the message. Adrian was vicious, and not someone to provoke. No one dared try again.

On the fifth day, the thunder and lightning finally stopped, and the acid rain eased a great deal. But the black sludge left behind on the ground would suddenly ignite. Flames burst up in patches, and whenever fresh acid rain landed on them, it was as though petrol had been poured over the fire. Sparks spat and burst in bright chains.

By then Evelyn had all but given up. She lay flat on her bed and barely moved. Apart from getting up to eat, she spent nearly all her time lying there like a corpse.

Adrian knew she was afraid, though he had no idea how to comfort her. All he could do was sit beside the bed and keep her company in silence.

“It’s the fifth day,” she said quietly. “All that acid water downstairs has already seeped into the soil.”

She raked a hand through her tangled hair and stared at him with swollen eyes.

“Will it stop?”

“It will.”

She gave a faint, brittle smile. “Then tell me. How many more days?”

Adrian looked out of the window and blinked. “Five at most.”

Evelyn sighed. “Let’s hope so.”

On the sixth day, the rain turned violent again. The drops were nearly the size of quail eggs, and each one hit the glass with a crack loud enough to make her flinch.

On the seventh day, it weakened again.

Then it intensified.

Then it weakened.

Back and forth, over and over, until everyone was left raw with nerves.

On the morning of the eighth day, the acid rain stopped. Just as people began to believe the crisis was over, it came crashing down again without warning, catching everyone off guard and frightening them badly enough that no one dared go outside to check anything after that.

On the ninth day, Evelyn stood quietly at the window. The base looked like the aftermath of a war. Everywhere she looked was ruin. Not a single patch remained intact. Every outer wall of the apartment blocks had been eaten away, and she suspected the foundations were no longer sound either.

On the tenth day, the acid rain stopped.

But no one believed it had truly ended.

The sky was still black and lightless. Steam and thick vapour still rose from the ground below, and now and then little tongues of fire still flickered out of the corroded holes in the earth.

Morning passed into evening.

A whole day went by without a single drop of acid rain.

Only then did people finally begin to believe that it was really over.

Yet not one face showed relief.

There was only fear, and a deep, unshakable dread.
