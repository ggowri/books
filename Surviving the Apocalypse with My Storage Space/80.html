Chapter 80 Surviving Alone: Part 4

Evelyn decided to line the outside of the cabin with red bricks and seal it with cement. That would keep rain and melting snow from seeping inside. The floor also needed to sit higher than the surrounding ground. Elevation meant dryness.

Once the logs were cut, she began digging the foundation. She planned for twenty five square meters. Inside, the layout would include a small bedroom, a compact kitchen, and a simple washroom.

Excavating the foundation alone took a full day and a half.

She spread crushed stone evenly across the base, then planted fir logs upright at every corner to serve as pillars. Each post had a carved notch. Between every two vertical posts, she stacked horizontal logs, fitting the notches together so the beams locked tightly in place. Layer by layer, she built upward.

For the horizontal beams, she chose slimmer logs. Anything too thick would be impossible for her to lift alone. She calculated the number of beams she needed, cut them to precise lengths, and stacked them neatly so construction would go more smoothly.

In rural houses, large triangular braces were often used to secure the main beam during construction. Evelyn studied the building manual carefully. But she did not have the strength to raise a single massive beam on her own. Instead, she substituted several smaller support beams, each about the width of her fist, and secured them one by one.

It took her five days to complete the main structural frame.

She only had one requirement for this house. It had to be practical. Appearance did not matter. What mattered was strength. It had to withstand anything.

On the sixth day, she pulled out thick waterproof plastic sheeting and draped it over the top beams, securing it tightly with nails and wooden strips. The tarp was dense, able to block both rain and light.

But that was not enough.

On top of the tarp, she laid down wooden boards in tight formation. Then she added another layer of waterproof sheeting.

With that many layers, rain would never get through. Even snow would have trouble accumulating, given the roof’s smooth surface and angled slope.

In the kitchen area, she built a ventilation pipe and chimney. Without proper metal piping, she constructed the chimney from red bricks instead.

The section extending above the roofline was angled outward slightly. That way, rain and snow would not fall directly into the opening.

Once the outer frame was mostly finished, she turned her attention to the interior flooring.

She leveled the dirt with a shovel, tamping it down firmly. Then she spread crushed stone, followed by another layer of packed soil. After compressing it again until it was solid and even, she laid thick wooden planks over the top.

Evelyn realized something during those days of labor.

Human potential was limitless.

Push hard enough, and you could do almost anything.

The cabin was starting to look real now. But there was still a fireplace to build, a fire pit, a sink and drainage for the kitchen, a stove, and a drain for the bathroom.

One step at a time.

The weather was getting colder, but there was no need to panic.

She had cut far more fir logs than she needed. Outside the cabin, she built a five step staircase. With the remaining wood, she erected a perimeter fence around the house. It still lacked a proper gate, but she would deal with that once the house was fully finished.

On the seventh day, she began constructing the fireplace.

Truthfully, she had never even seen one in real life. She could only rely on the diagrams in her book and figure it out as she went.

The fireplace required its own chimney.

After two days of trial and error, she finally completed it. It looked simple in design but had been incredibly difficult to build.

It was not pretty. The red bricks and cement had not fully dried yet.

She did not care.

Ugly or not, it would work.

On the seventh day, Evelyn started building the fireplace. Honestly, she had never even seen one in real life. All she could do was study the pictures in her book and figure it out through trial and error.

A fireplace needed its own chimney. After two days of adjustments and rebuilding, she finally completed one. It looked simple, but it had been incredibly difficult to construct. It was not pretty. The red bricks and cement had not fully dried yet. Evelyn decided she did not care. It would have to do.

Over the past few days, Sprig had grown noticeably sulky. Evelyn no longer had time to play. She worked from the moment she woke up and went straight to bed after washing up at night. The cycle repeated day after day. The squirrel would occasionally wander in front of her, only to be shooed away.

Evelyn held out a roasted pheasant drumstick toward it.

Sprig pretended not to see it. With a flick of its tail, it turned its back on her.

"You're not even going to eat pheasant? I roasted this just for you."

The squirrel swished its tail again and ignored her.

"Fine. Don't eat it. Don't talk to me either. From now on, we're in a cold war. If you won't pay attention to me, I won't pay attention to you."

Evelyn snorted deliberately at its back and ate the drumstick herself.

Sprig's ears twitched. Then its tail twitched. Finally, it turned around cautiously. When it saw the pheasant was gone, it squealed and lunged at her to snatch it.

Evelyn refused to indulge it. After eating her fill, she went back to work, leaving the little squirrel sulking in place.

When she was bored, she would pull out her old MP3 player and listen to music. Strangely enough, despite living in the mountains for so many days, she had not encountered a single wild animal.

Had they all gone into hibernation?

It was November.

Yes, winter had arrived.

In the final days of finishing the cabin, the temperature dropped to five or six degrees. The fog in the mountains remained thick. Beyond ten meters, everything blurred into gray.

The stove in the kitchen was complete now. She could boil water and cook inside. She often kept a fire going to dry out the dampness in the cabin.

The exterior bricks and cement still had not been applied. Evelyn worried that rain might come before snow. She pushed herself to speed up reinforcing the outer walls.

After days of constant work and handling water, her hands turned red and swollen. At night, the itching drove her crazy. Ointment did nothing. In the end, she gave herself two injections to calm the inflammation.

Laying bricks and smoothing cement took several more days. The weather was gloomy, and the cement dried slowly. Evelyn built several fires in the yard to help speed up the process.

On November eleventh, a few scattered snowflakes drifted down from the sky.

The cabin was finally finished inside and out. It was still slightly damp and not ready to move into immediately. Evelyn lit fires in the main room, the fireplace, and the heated brick bed. The insulation worked well. Once the doors and windows were shut, the interior felt warm and secure.

To celebrate finishing the cabin, she decided on a proper feast.

Lamb stew. Fish in chili bean sauce. Cabbage and tofu soup. Braised eggplant. Stewed pork.

It took three hours to prepare everything.

This was the best meal she had eaten since the apocalypse began.

Even better, she and Sprig had made up. After their brief cold war, they were back to being close friends, planning to sleep side by side again.

Evelyn sat inside the RV, gazing at the small yard and the little wooden cabin beyond it.

Snowflakes began to swirl down, mixed with a soft drizzle. The lights inside the vehicle glowed warm and golden. Sprig sat nearby, watching cartoons on a tablet.

Evelyn sipped her soup while watching the snow fall.

What should she do tomorrow?

Build a proper gate for the fence. Improve the pond. The water in her storage space needed to be preserved. The pond water here was clear and clean, even purer than city tap water.

And the day after tomorrow?

Right.

The wild buffalo stored in her space needed to be processed. Drain the blood. Skin them. Cut the meat.

She could make beef jerky or braised beef.
