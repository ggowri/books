Chapter 28 Rainstorm, Toads: Part 3

Preparing toads was actually simple. First, extract the toad venom, then remove all the internal organs. After that, the body cavity needed to be split open and left to dry in the sun. But now, with no sunlight and temperatures plummeting, that was impossible. Using a generator to dry them would waste precious fuel and power. So for now, Evelyn had to put the idea on hold.

Though toads were technically edible, their numbers had exploded into a full-on infestation within just a few days. It was now nearly impossible to find a place to even set foot outside.

Claire had started burning parts of her house—wardrobes, wooden bed frames. All the greenery outside had already been chopped down. People were beginning to break into abandoned units, dragging out any wooden furniture they could find just to have something to burn. But with the arrival of the toads, going out had become nearly impossible. One resident from the neighboring building tried and ended up being swarmed by toads leaping from the water. They slipped, fell in, and were swept away.

The internal flooding was only getting worse. Calvin brought back more bad news: Elaris's main river bridge had collapsed. Several residential areas had started experiencing ground collapses too.

The toads were a double-edged sword. While they brought chaos, they also brought food.
Thanks to them, some had finally solved their hunger problems.

Evelyn didn't stay idle either. She started going out with a bucket to collect toads.

Over in Block A, a woman who'd recently given birth had nearly died. Evelyn had checked on her during her recovery—the husband was attentive, and the two children were being cared for surprisingly well.

What surprised Evelyn most was the number of pregnant women in the community. Quite a few had gotten pregnant even after the disaster began. But with malnutrition, freezing conditions, and constant stress, miscarriages were tragically common.

As the rain poured on and the temperatures dropped further, a cold wave swept through the city. People could no longer go outside. Even with full protective gear, the wind cut through like knives. Evelyn could feel the pain down to her bones.

The wind outside howled like a ghost. Her eyes were burning painfully.

She'd just come back from foraging toads when a blast of frigid air hit her face. Her eyes immediately started watering nonstop.

The freezing temperatures left her no choice—she had to turn the heater on early. Her fingers were already red and swollen, itchy and inflamed. Her toes were starting to crack and peel.

Evelyn soaked her feet in hot water, applied half a tube of ointment, then used a warm compress on her eyes. Only then did the stinging begin to ease.

After resting for half an hour, her body finally started to warm up again. She brought the two buckets of toads to the balcony and quickly began cleaning them.

But by the third day of the cold wave, people started developing reactions to the frigid air—rashes, sudden skin allergies, rhinitis, and more.

Evelyn stopped going outside altogether.

Her first priority was survival. Only once she was safe and stable could she worry about helping others.

She ran the heater five hours a day. The rest of the time, she endured using only residual warmth. She layered three soundproof panels in the living room and pulled out a jump rope, exercising indoors just to stay warm.

Two days later, the rain abruptly stopped.

A tear split open the gray clouds, and a blinding shaft of light pierced through the sky.
The sun came out.

Evelyn looked up at it through the window. But instead of joy, a terrible sense of foreboding filled her chest.

The sun only lasted for three hours.

But in that time, everything changed.

The rain stopped. The thunder disappeared. The cold wind vanished. Even the toads retreated into the water.

People were ecstatic. They screamed from balconies, shouted from stairwells, even ran out into the streets, convinced the worst had finally passed.

Many seized the opportunity to go out and scavenge for supplies and firewood.

But after just thirty minutes outside, their eyes and skin began to burn.

Three hours later, the sun vanished as quickly as it had come—like it had never been there at all.

And the people who had gone out?

Their lips turned purple. They began vomiting and suffering from diarrhea. Their skin swelled red, their eyes poured tears uncontrollably.

Then came the pounding at Evelyn's door.

Desperate wails echoed from the hallway as people begged her to help them.

Fully geared up, Evelyn grabbed her knife and toolbox and opened the door.

When she saw the first patient being carried over, her eyes flashed with a rare shock.

After a quick examination, her initial diagnosis was grim but clear: They had been poisoned by the sun.

One after another, more people were carried over by their families, and Evelyn began to observe a clearer pattern in this so-called sun poisoning.

It started with burns—skin swelling from red to black, followed by rapid ulceration. Hair falling out in thick clumps. Eyes red and swollen, constantly tearing. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea. Irregular heartbeat. Confusion and delirium.

Evelyn had no cure.

She had some burn ointments, but they weren't made for this. And even if she had enough to share, she couldn't afford to take them out. The severity of the burns made it obvious—ordinary creams were useless here.

Bluntly put, anyone poisoned by the sun was as good as dead.

When Evelyn told them she couldn't help, the families broke down in the hallway, wailing in grief.

Most of the poisoned were young and strong.

One man from the eighth floor collapsed not long after exposure. He started foaming at the mouth, then vomiting blood. Within an hour, he was gone.

Evelyn turned to leave quietly, hoping to slip away.

But an old man grabbed her arm.

Then he dropped to his knees and began slamming his forehead against the ground, bowing over and over.

The repeatedly banging of his head against the floor made Evelyn flinch. She instinctively stepped back two paces.

The old man was clutching his son, his eyes hollow and filled with despair.

"Please… save him. He's my only child. If I lose him, I have nothing left. Doctor, I'm begging you. Don't give up on him. He's still breathing. There's still a chance!"

Evelyn inhaled deeply, her throat tight. She shook her head.

"It's my fault… He went out to find food for me. If it weren't for that, he wouldn't have gone… Jonah, please, don't die. Open your eyes, just once, look at me."

The young man began coughing blood again, violently. Using the last of his strength, he lifted a trembling hand and gently wiped his father's tears.

Then his arm dropped like a stone. He was gone.

The old man stared blankly at his son's face, frozen for a second before his scream shattered the hallway silence.

The others fell into grief. Raw, helpless, soul-deep grief. That pain of watching someone die slowly, knowing there was nothing you could do.

Some came to beg Evelyn again. Others lashed out at her, blaming her.

Evelyn drew her knife and took another step back, pressing herself against her door. She watched, stunned, as the hallway filled with weeping and despair.

The old man eventually carried his son away.

But halfway down the return trip, he climbed onto his motor raft, clutched his son's body close… and jumped into the floodwaters.

The water swallowed them whole.

*****

After the sun poisoning, fear crept into every home.

For days, no one dared leave the building. Everyone was terrified the sun might suddenly return—or worse, that something even more unpredictable might happen.

Calvin's father Max developed acute hand eczema from the cold. After examining him, Evelyn explained that it was a kind of inflammatory and itchy skin condition. The hands would break out in red patches and blisters, which made people want to scratch until they bled.

Thankfully, Calvin had returned from one of his last scavenging trips with a decent batch of medicine, including calamine lotion.

When she got home, Evelyn immediately sprayed herself down with disinfectant. Eczema wasn't contagious, but she couldn't afford to take any chances.

But over the next few days, cases began to spike, with more and more people showing signs of eczema. The strange part was that it wasn't just hands anymore. The locations were random and varied.

The condition mostly affected women and the elderly.

And for some women… it wasn't on their hands at all.

Many had developed painful rashes in their private areas.

Evelyn didn't know how to respond to those cases. She felt completely out of her depth.

Then came the next shock.

The toads that had disappeared during the brief sunlight returned the very next day.

But this time… something about them was different.
