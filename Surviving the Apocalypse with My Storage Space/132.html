Chapter 132 Night Encounter with the Mountain Monkey

“This rain is really heavy. I wonder when it will stop.”

Adrian glanced at the downpour outside the window, expression calm.

“It should clear by morning,” he said.

“That’s a relief. We’ve been out for half a month. In another two weeks, we’ll need to head back.”

Evelyn’s instincts were sharp. She knew the next disaster was approaching.

Around midnight, a sudden pounding rattled the glass. Evelyn switched on the light and froze. Outside the window crouched a strange creature.

It looked like a monkey, yet not quite. Like a gorilla, but even stranger.

Its head was large and elongated, with bulging growths on either side of its nose, vertical stripes running alongside, and a strip of bright red skin down the centre. A sharp tuft protruded from the top of its head. Its fur had a faint greenish tinge, as did its face.

“That’s a mountain monkey,” Adrian whispered beside her.

A mountain monkey? The legendary kind from the old tales—more feared than wolves?

According to ancient texts, the mountain monkey is a one-legged spirit that lives in the mountains, ugly, human-faced, with long arms.

Now, dozens of them had gathered outside the RV.

If they had been ordinary monkeys or gorillas, it might have been manageable. But mountain monkeys were the largest primates in the world, omnivorous and highly aggressive, often predatory.

“Drive. Take the crossbow. If they attack, we strike back.”

Evelyn reached for the driver’s seat, but the monkeys were already swarming over the roof and hood, hopping and slapping the glass, mouths open in challenge.

She ignored them and started the engine. Maneuvering safely with such heavy, bouncing creatures on the vehicle was no easy task.

“Adrian, gather them together and spray them with insecticide.”

Adrian caught the bottle she tossed him and whistled. Instantly, the monkeys congregated at the windows.

He opened the window and sprayed. Evelyn seized the moment, accelerating and leaving them behind.

The insecticide stung the monkeys’ eyes, making them howl and roll on the ground. A few chased the RV, and two leapt onto the roof, hammering it with their fists.

“They’re amusing,” Evelyn muttered. “Teasing us, banging on windows, jumping on the roof. And yet, when we fight back, they’re offended.”

She pushed the accelerator, trying to shake the roof riders. Suddenly, one ran into the road ahead. Evelyn hit it, crushing it under the RV. The others screeched in protest, then gave chase.

“Go on ahead. I’ll deal with them,” Adrian shouted.

“They’re primates, smart enough to outthink us. Don’t get out. I’ll drive us clear in this rain.”

Fortunately, the road was clear. After nearly three kilometres, Evelyn noticed the monkeys still following, picking up stones and battering the RV.

Her patience snapped. She hadn’t expected such relentless persistence.

“Adrian, shoot the one at the front. Set an example.”

He opened the window, aiming the crossbow at the lead monkey—a black-faced one. It seemed to command the others.

A single arrow pierced its skull, and it collapsed. The others wailed around it and paused. Evelyn used the moment to speed away.

Twenty minutes later, the road was blocked. Evelyn tossed Adrian a raincoat and stowed the RV in the space. Carrying Pip and Lark, they ran into the nearby forest.

“There’s a cave. We can hide here until the rain stops at dawn.”

Adrian carried Evelyn inside. The cave was dry, a perfect refuge.

“I didn’t expect to encounter mountain monkeys. Could the disaster be early? They’ve come down from the deep mountains,” she said, watching the storm from the cave entrance.

Adrian lit a fire. Pip and Lark, accustomed to the journey, were energetic.

By seven in the morning, the rain had stopped. Evelyn and Adrian left the cave and decided to head back.

The air smelled of wet earth. Evelyn had barely walked a few minutes when she stopped, examining a leaf carefully.

“What is it?” Adrian asked, noticing her pale expression.

“There’s a hole,” she said. “A corroded hole.”

Her hands trembled as she held his.

“I know what’s coming next.”

“Acid rain. Black acid rain, as corrosive as sulfuric acid. Adrian, we have to get back to the base immediately.”

He looked at the leaf in her hand, then picked a few more, checking each one. Some bore corroded holes, others were intact.

The storm last night had contained traces of acid rain—a warning from nature.

In her previous life, acid rain had lasted seven days, killing countless people. It didn’t kill instantly, but contact with skin caused rapid corrosion, flesh rotting inch by inch, untreatable.

“Do you trust me?” she asked.

“Of course,” he said.

Without hesitation, he lifted her onto his back. Pip and Lark were placed in a cloth bag, held close by Evelyn. He ran through the forest at full speed. Listening to his heartbeat, Evelyn’s anxiety gradually eased.

After a full day and night, they returned to the base. Showing their resident IDs, they were let in immediately.

Back in the apartment building, Evelyn and Adrian found Officer Shaw and asked about what had happened during their absence.

“Has the wheat been harvested yet?” she asked.

Shaw shook his head. “Not yet. It isn’t ripe. They say it’ll take another two weeks.”

Two weeks?

There wasn’t time. The acid rain was imminent.

That evening, Evelyn wrote a warning sign and asked Adrian to hang it outside Administration Hall. But the staff mistook it for a prank and destroyed it before anyone could see.

“Adrian, we’ll go to the broadcast station tonight. We need to warn everyone. No more deaths. Acid rain is worse than the parasites. Save whoever we can.”

Evelyn had trained in bel canto as a child; altering her voice was simple.

Thousands had already died from the parasite disaster. Those weren’t numbers—they were lives.

Without Adrian, she wouldn’t dare risk it. But with him, she knew they could act safely.
