Chapter 61 Heatwave, Mid-Autumn

As the winds intensified, going outside became nearly impossible.

Everyone stayed indoors.

Gales hurled sand against the windows. The glass was now so thickly coated in grit that they could no longer see outside. Calvin led the group in reinforcing the building's security door downstairs. They nailed wooden boards over the hallway windows and their own apartment windows as well.

The gauze they had scavenged was repurposed into makeshift head coverings. Without them, stepping outside was impossible. The air was suffocating and hot. Sand blew into their collars, mixing with sweat. The salt from their own bodies stung every inch of skin like brine.

Evelyn checked the air quality index.

API 200.

She had to seriously consider leaving.

But where could they go besides the base?

She pulled out a national map.

Southwest or northwest might be safer. There was one critical factor to eliminate. No nuclear power plants nearby.

The southwest and northwest regions met that condition.

That night, she lay in bed listening to the wind howl outside.

Buried vehicles were lifted and rolled by the storm. Glass from the building next door shattered under the pressure, sharp cracking sounds echoing in the darkness.

She barely slept.

When she woke, dark circles shadowed her eyes.

Still, the routine continued.

Cook. Steam buns. Simmer soups. Once finished, store everything in her space.

She did not feel tired.

Seeing the growing stockpile filled her with a deep, steady calm.

The cactus had sprouted two new pads.

Several other houseplants had withered beyond saving as temperatures climbed higher.

By the time Mid-Autumn Festival arrived, Evelyn took out six cans of meat and half a pound of bean sprouts she had grown and headed upstairs to Calvin's apartment.

The others were already there.

When she set the meat cans down, Eddie nearly burst into tears.

"I haven't seen meat in months."

"Eddie, don't you hunt snakes? Snake meat tastes good too," Ray said, sniffing the braised pork can with longing.

"Snake not eating me was already a blessing," Eddie replied. "During that time, I hid at home and didn't dare step outside. I waited until most of the snakes were gone before I ventured out. I used to have a cat. When the toads swarmed, it got sick and died. After that, I survived on cat food."

A flicker of sadness crossed his eyes.

The others were about to comfort him when he suddenly grinned.

"Cat food has meat too. Doesn't taste great, but it's nutritious. Only downside, I've been constipated ever since."

Everyone fell silent.

"Let's just throw everything into one big stew," Calvin suggested, setting an iron pot over a small fire.

They immediately stepped back.

It was already unbearably hot. Adding fire to the mix felt like punishment.

"Boil water for the noodles first," Evelyn said, taking charge. "Add the bean sprouts. Don't dump the canned meat in the pot. It's too salty."

She placed a stainless-steel basin on the table and broke the instant noodles into it. The bean sprouts were already washed and ready to cook.

There was also a bottle of chili sauce and some sausage on the table.

Evelyn handled the cooking while the others assisted.

The moment she opened a can of braised pork, the rich aroma filled the room.

Even Wendy stepped closer, eyes wide.

Evelyn smiled and picked up a piece for her.

Before the apocalypse, Evelyn had stockpiled plenty of canned goods, but none of them tasted as good as the ones she found at the food factory.

The meat and fruit cans were fine.

Seafood cans, though, Evelyn still could not get used to.

On the table sat a few snow rice cakes that Eddie had brought up.

"No mooncakes, so we'll make do with these," he said. "One each."

Ray and Evan held theirs carefully, reluctant to eat them too quickly.

Evelyn poured the cooked bean sprouts and broth into a basin. The instant noodles softened almost immediately.

"I used to think instant noodles were junk food," Eddie said after taking a sip of soup. He let out a satisfied sigh. "Now I know how good they are."

Wendy squatted beside Evelyn, clutching a small bowl and sipping quietly.

Calvin placed a piece of meat into her bowl.

She refused to eat it.

Evelyn thought it might be too salty and suggested rinsing it in clean water first. But even then, Wendy shook her head.

"She probably wants Calvin to eat it," Eddie said softly.

Everyone froze.

Calvin's eyes reddened instantly as he pulled Wendy into his arms.

The room felt heavy.

Their first Mid-Autumn Festival after the apocalypse passed like that.

Before heading home, Evelyn slipped a few candies into Wendy's pocket.

When the temperature climbed to 55 degrees, the API rose past 220.

No matter how carefully they rationed, Eddie and the others only had about twenty days of drinking water left.

Late at night, distant streaks of firelight flickered on the horizon.

Another day crossed off the calendar.

Another day survived.

Eddie had been experimenting with condensation water collection lately. Judging from his expression, success had not come yet.

Their injuries had mostly healed.

They had planned to retaliate against the group that ambushed them, but the raging winds forced them to postpone.

Recently, a strange plant had begun sprouting downstairs.

It resembled plantain weed, but carried a faint fragrance. When its leaves were sliced open, droplets of moisture seeped out.

This was where Eddie's expertise finally mattered.

He smelled it. Touched it. Even licked it.

After brushing off the sand, he chewed a leaf and waited.

Half an hour passed with no side effects.

"It's edible," he declared.

"When the wind dies down, we'll go forage."

Eddie believed it was some kind of wild vegetable. He did not know its name, but the juice was mildly sweet and it tolerated extreme heat.

"It's like the sky cracked open a window for us," he said.

"I've observed the wind patterns," Evelyn added. "Between ten p.m. and two a.m., it's at its weakest. That's our window."

With edible greens in sight, everyone's spirits lifted.

At ten that night, each of them grabbed a bucket and shovel and headed out.

The plants were scattered, one every few yards, small and sparse, about the size of baby spinach.

Wearing night vision goggles, Evelyn stepped out of the complex with her bucket.

After an hour, she had only gathered a handful.

"Looks like God doesn't close every door on us," Eddie murmured as he came up beside her.

"The ground's completely desertified. I thought nothing would grow again. But here we are."

Evelyn glanced at his basin.

A pitiful few leaves lay inside.

"Keep reflecting like that and it'll be two a.m. before you know it."

"I'm nearsighted," he complained. "And it's pitch black. Is that night vision goggles you're wearing? Got a spare pair? Can I borrow one?"
