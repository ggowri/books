Chapter 104 Fishing on the Way

At five the next morning, they left the village on the dot and carried on.

After drinking Adrian’s blood, Evelyn found that her stamina had improved remarkably overnight. By the end of the day, the two of them had covered nearly twenty-five kilometres.

On the eighth day, they reached Silvermere River.

The river ran across three provinces, and several stretches of it had always been dangerous. During the torrential rains, the sluice gates had collapsed and several downstream cities were swallowed by floodwater. Then came the extreme heat, the river shrank away, and the embankments split open. After the earthquake, landslides from the mountains on both banks had poured into the river, choking whole sections of it.

“It’s frozen solid. The ice is thick enough. We can cross it slowly.”

Evelyn picked up a rock and hurled it hard at the frozen surface. A thin crack appeared, but the ice did not cave in. That reassured her a good deal.

The river was astonishingly wide, stretching so far it almost seemed endless. Evelyn took out a bag of soil from her space, scooped up a handful, and scattered it over the ice.

“We can walk over that. It’ll stop us slipping.”

Adrian seemed to find the idea rather foolish. “I’ll carry you across. You won’t fall.”

Evelyn smiled with exaggerated modesty. “Oh, honestly, asking you again. I do feel awful about it.”

She did not look remotely embarrassed. In one swift motion she put the soil away, dusted off her gloves, and beamed at him.

“Well then, thank you very much. Crouch a little, would you? You’re too tall. I can’t jump that high.”

Adrian glanced at her, grabbed her one-handed, and dumped her onto his back with all the tenderness of a sack of grain.

The cloth bag carrying Pip and Lark hung at the front, and Evelyn was on his back. He tested the ice with a step, then began crossing the river.

Evelyn wiped the moisture from her lashes and gripped his shoulders tightly with both hands. When they were nearly halfway across, she suddenly noticed fish moving beneath the ice.

She rubbed her eyes, hardly believing it.

“Adrian, can you see the fish under the ice?”

“Yes.”

For a moment Evelyn had thought she must be imagining it. “Does this count as a genetic mutation, then?”

“Yes.”

She pressed her lips together in a pleased smile. “Fancy grilled fish?”

Adrian stopped dead.

He looked down at the ice with complete seriousness. Then he let go, and Evelyn dropped straight onto it. She was bundled up in thick layers, but it still hurt.

“What was that for?”

“Fishing.”

He turned to her with an utterly solemn expression. “Give me the shovel.”

Evelyn scrambled painfully upright. “Were you starved to death in a previous life or something?” Even as she grumbled, she still took out the shovel and handed it over.

Adrian accepted it and immediately began smashing through the ice. Before long he had hacked open a hole. Evelyn whipped out a landing net and hurried over, eager to scoop up fish, only to skid and go down onto her knees.

Resting one hand on the shovel, Adrian looked down at her. A trace of puzzlement crossed his mismatched eyes.

“There’s no need.”

Evelyn had only just begun to stand when she heard him say it coolly.

No need for what?

“I slipped, that’s all. I’m not kneeling to thank you, so don’t get the wrong idea.”

One of his brows twitched faintly. “Hopeless.”

Then he simply hauled her up and deposited her farther away.

He took the landing net from her, lowered it into the hole, and with one lift brought up two enormous bighead carp, each weighing around ten kilos. The moment Evelyn saw them, all trace of her earlier embarrassment vanished. She hastily pulled out a large basket and, once the fish were dead, dropped them in.

“Ha, there’s even a little whitebait.” It was no bigger than two fingers, but she picked it up all the same.

For the first time, Evelyn truly understood the joy of winter fishing. Even that trip in the mountains before had not felt quite like this.

“Not bad at all. There’s carp, silver carp…”

“Adrian, this one could be steamed, this one braised, this one fried, this one made into soup.”

He lifted his head and looked at her in silence. Evelyn assumed she had been talking too much and quickly mimed zipping her mouth shut.

Then he said, “We should blow the ice open.”

Evelyn stared at him.

Did he really have to be so savage about everything?

“No. If you blow the ice apart, you’ll blow the fish to bits too.”

Adrian turned serious at once. “Then we keep netting them.”

What had started as a difficult journey suddenly became a supply run. Once they had filled two large baskets, Evelyn pulled every empty basin and bucket she had out of her space.

“Adrian, I’m going back to the bank to set up camp first.”

She suddenly remembered she might have a skateboard and some skates tucked away in her space. After a quick rummage, she found one.

“I really do have a skateboard.”

She stepped onto it, did a cautious little trial loop, and then glided off towards the riverbank.

By then Adrian was already hacking out a second hole. For him, fishing itself held no charm. But every time he looked at the catch, he thought of Evelyn’s braised fish, steamed fish, sweet and sour fish. That was enough to make him keen in an instant.

Evelyn fell twice on the way, though fortunately she landed on her backside both times and did not come to too much harm. She had already brought Pip and Lark over with her. By the time she reached the bank, her eyelashes were damp again, so she quickly wiped away the mist, found a flat patch of ground, set up the tent, and got a fire going.

Silvermere River lay low between the land on either side, so the freezing wind funnelled straight through it. The cold felt sharper for it. Even so, Evelyn was in far better shape than before. Her energy seemed endless, as though she could keep going forever.

She set water to boil and put on a pot of mutton stew beside it. Mutton drove out the cold, and winter was the right season for it.

After shutting the two little creatures inside the tent and turning on the heater for them, Evelyn climbed back onto the skateboard and returned to the ice.

“Another basket already. Oh, look at these. All topmouth culter. Best steamed.”

The moment Adrian heard that, his hands moved even faster. Standing behind him, Evelyn covered her mouth and laughed.

After four solid hours of work, she suggested they rest for one hour, warm themselves by the fire on the bank, eat something to restore their strength, and drink a bit of ginger tea to ward off the cold.

Adrian had no objection. But the fish they had caught needed to be put into her space first. Two crates were still full of live ones. Evelyn watched as he picked them up one by one and knocked them against a brick. Each fish rolled its eyes and died on the spot.

Working together, they quickly sorted the crates and stored everything away. Looking at the steadily growing pile of supplies, Evelyn’s eyes brimmed with satisfaction.

“Well done, well done. I’ve got the mutton ready. Come on then, Adrian the Butcher.”

“Adrian the Butcher?”

Evelyn nodded, then rubbed her chin thoughtfully. “No good? All right then, Adrian the Fisherman.”

She burst out laughing and shot off across the ice on her skateboard. Adrian followed behind, carrying the shovel over one shoulder.

“The mutton’s ready. Start with a bowl of broth. It’ll warm you through. Radish in winter, ginger in summer. I put radish in it, so it’ll help fight the cold.”

She handed him a bowl. Adrian took a sip, and his eyes lit up at once.

Evelyn cradled her own bowl and drank as well. Instant bliss.

“How many fish do you think there are in this river?” she asked.

“How many containers have you got for them?”

Evelyn gave a sly little grin. “Plenty. The question is whether the fish will last.”

“Then we catch every last one.”

A flash of fierce determination passed through Adrian’s eyes. Not determination for survival, but for braised fish and steamed fish.

With a solemn expression, Evelyn clenched her fist and gave him a proper gesture of encouragement.
