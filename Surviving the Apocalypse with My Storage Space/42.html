Chapter 42 Heatwave, Snake Infestation: Part 6

For Evelyn, knitting had become something she grew more determined about the more she failed. Slowly, she even began to enjoy it. Her personality was a little stubborn. When she did something, she either gave up completely or committed to doing it perfectly. She never quit halfway.

This time, she decided to crochet a scarf. After all, a scarf was much simpler than a sweater.

With the failure of the first attempt behind her, the second try felt noticeably smoother. She straightened out the red yarn and wound it into a neat ball, planning to leave a bit of fringe at the ends. Once Evelyn found her rhythm, she became completely absorbed. From morning to night, she barely ate or rested. By evening, a not quite perfect scarf was finished. She put it away with quiet satisfaction.

Before going to sleep, Evelyn set herself a small goal.

Tomorrow, she would learn how to knit socks.

In the middle of the night, Evelyn heard the sound of a child crying.

She opened her eyes, stared blankly for a moment, then turned on the bedside lamp. Taking her binoculars and flashlight, she walked to the window.

The crying came from the building next door. Sometimes it was loud, sometimes faint. It sounded like a little girl.

Evelyn raised the binoculars and looked outside. A black snake was still hanging from the railing. Several of the metal bars had already been snapped by the snakes that climbed up to keep watch on her. Only two bars remained, tightly wrapped by the black snake's tail.

Ignoring the snake, Evelyn focused on the crying, trying to pinpoint its source. At that moment, a strong gust of wind howled past, nearly blowing the snake off. Evelyn did not believe in ghosts or spirits, but even so, a chill crept into her heart.

Thinking about it, how many people were still alive in this building?

Maybe… only her.

Bodies had been burned on the rooftop. More than a dozen corpses lay on the sixth floor. Upstairs and downstairs, she had lost count.

Familiar faces. Strangers. Friendly ones. People she disliked.

All of them had died in this building.

There was no movement in the binoculars. The crying came and went. A deep sense of dread settled in Evelyn's chest. She knew all too well that without law or civilization, humans were no different from other species. In fact, they might be even more terrifying and cruel.

Just like big snakes devouring smaller ones, people were the same.

In corners no one could see, perhaps humans had already begun preying on one another. And women and children sat at the very bottom of the food chain.

They were called two-legged sheep.

The crying sounded even more piercing and hopeless in the dead of night. Evelyn stared at the pitch black sky, her heart shrouded in a heavy veil of darkness.

Gradually, the crying stopped. The wind outside died down as well.

Evelyn returned to bed, lying there motionless, staring blankly at the lamp by her pillow.

*****

The next day, Evelyn carried on as usual.

She practiced knitting socks, read books, wrote in her journal, exercised, and occasionally picked up a Rubik's Cube to train her hands and mind. The days passed in monotonous repetition.

Outside, the temperature had already climbed to forty degrees.

The water level dropped to the fourth floor. More skeletons were exposed, along with cars, kayaks, and inflatable boats that had been swept down from upstream by the flood.

Snakes were heat tolerant creatures, but forty degrees was already close to their limit.

Over the next few days, Evelyn kept observing the situation outside. On the fifth day, when the temperature hit forty two degrees, large numbers of snakes began to die.

Snakes from the upper floors fell after dying, crashing straight into the railings outside the windows. The last two remaining bars could not take the weight and finally snapped.

Snake corpses rotted extremely fast. Under normal conditions, it took three to five hours. In this heat, it was even quicker.

The stench outside was overwhelming, like standing inside a septic tank. Even indoors, Evelyn had to wear a mask to avoid breathing it in.

She scratched the center of her palm. A small blister burst under her nails. The broken skin was itchy and painful. She scratched again. The skin on her palm began to wrinkle and peel. Frowning, Evelyn tore the loose skin away, but the more she pulled, the worse it got. Soon, the entire surface of her palm looked mangled.

She glanced toward the window and forced herself to walk over. On the railing of the building across the way, strips of shed snake skin were still hanging and fluttering in the wind. Evelyn looked down at her palm, then froze.

She hurriedly dug out a tube of ointment from her space, squeezed out a huge amount, and smeared it all over her palm and the back of her hand. She tried to reassure herself. Peeling skin on the hands was normal. It could be an allergic reaction, or a fungal infection like hand eczema. Nothing serious. Ointment would fix it.

But seeing those strips of snake skin flapping outside the window still sent a chill down her spine.

It was the first time Evelyn realized how unbearable itching could be. She wanted to scratch the raw skin so badly. One scratch would bring instant relief. But common sense told her not to. Scratching would only make it worse. She was going crazy. At one point, she even wanted to chop the whole hand off. The itch spread through every nerve in her body. Gritting her teeth, she wrapped her right hand tightly.

The ointment burned as soon as it touched her inflamed skin. To prevent further infection, Evelyn sprayed disinfectant all over the apartment, soaking every corner.

After using three full tubes of ointment and enduring five days of torment, her right hand finally healed. Aside from some redness and swelling, there was no broken skin or itching left.

Outside, the temperature had climbed to forty five degrees.

She splashed water onto the glass. With a sharp hiss, the droplets evaporated instantly.

Putting on sun protective clothing, a mask, and protective goggles, Evelyn opened the window. A wave of scorching heat slammed into her, leaving her dizzy.

Most of the snakes were dead now. Occasionally, a few large ones could still be seen writhing among the piles of corpses. They tried to climb the walls, but the moment they touched the burning hot surface, they fell back down.

The stench of rot pierced through her mask, making her brain feel starved of oxygen.

Evelyn added two more masks. Once she could no longer smell anything, she took out her binoculars and began scanning the entire residential complex.

There was nothing but silence.

Not the faintest sound.

Then she heard a hissing sound rising from the floodwaters below.

Evelyn leaned out to look. What she saw shocked her greatly.

A massive black python rose from the sea of dead snakes. Its wide, flattened head lifted high as it opened its gaping mouth and swallowed several snake corpses in one gulp. Evelyn could not tell how long its body was, but judging by its thickness alone, it had to be at least seven or eight meters long.

She slammed the window shut at once, pulled a crossbow from her space, and coated the arrowhead with poison. Pressing a hand over her wildly pounding heart, Evelyn leaned against the wall, closed her eyes, and slowly exhaled.

The python was enormous. It was nonvenomous, but humans were no match for it.

Her grip on the crossbow tightened until her fingertips turned white.

Suddenly, Evelyn thought of the people on the eleventh floor who had once knelt, begging God for forgiveness and divine protection.

If she could, she wanted to kneel too.

Not to God.

But to pray that the giant python would leave.

In the face of absolute power, Evelyn felt an overwhelming sense of frustration and helplessness.
